## 27.1 Concept Explanation

GenAI interview loops vary by company and seniority, but they cluster into a small number of categories: system design, coding, deployment and infra, MLOps, AI infrastructure, and scenario-based behavioral. This chapter prepares you for each.

A common interviewer mistake is testing pure ML theory. A common candidate mistake is showing pure ML theory without production reasoning. The intersection — production-grade GenAI engineering — is what is actually valued and what the rest of this book has prepared you for.

## 27.1a The AI-Engineer Interview Loop at Product Companies and AI Startups ➕

The categories above are topics. This section looks at the rounds you actually sit through, because each round is scored differently. Exact loops change between companies and over time, so treat this as the common shape and confirm the real loop with your recruiter. It is fair to ask "what rounds are there, and what does each one assess?".

### Screen: what they probe

**What interviewers probe.** Whether your experience matches the role's scope ("built a RAG feature" vs "owned an AI product end to end"), and whether you can explain one project in two minutes.

**Why.** The hiring manager is deciding whether to spend four or five interviewer-hours on you. They are listening for ownership and numbers, not keywords.

**How to answer well.** Prepare a 90-second pitch that follows the pattern problem, what you built, one hard part, one measured result. For example: "I built a real-time voice agent on LiveKit for inbound support calls. The hard part was turn-taking, because the agent kept interrupting people mid-sentence. I added a semantic end-of-turn model and tuned endpointing, which cut false interruptions and brought response latency down to about a second. It now handles a share of calls without a human." Replace the numbers with your own measured ones.

**Common mistakes.** Reciting your whole resume in order. Saying "we" so often that your own contribution disappears. Not asking what the team's biggest AI problem is right now. That question gives you material for every later round.

### Coding round: practical vs DSA

**What interviewers probe.** Product companies with a standard engineering ladder often still run one data-structures-and-algorithms round. AI startups more often give a practical task, such as "stream tokens from this mock LLM to the client, add retries, handle cancellation", "write a chunker that respects headings" or "implement top-k cosine similarity and deduplicate near-identical results".

**Why.** They want proof that you write correct async code under time pressure. LLM features fail in the plumbing (timeouts, partial streams, retries that duplicate side effects) much more often than in the model.

**How to answer well.** State the interface first (input, output, errors), write the happy path, then add the edge cases out loud: empty input, upstream timeout, client disconnect, a retry that is not idempotent. Section 27.10a has a full streaming endpoint to practise with, and 27.3a has a retrieval and ranking exercise.

**Common mistakes.** Writing silent code for 20 minutes. Ignoring cancellation. Using `Promise.all` over 10,000 items with no concurrency limit.

### LLM-app system design

**What interviewers probe.** Whether you can turn a vague product ("an AI assistant for our support team") into a design with a latency budget, a cost estimate, an evaluation plan and failure modes. Section 27.2 covers the method, and 27.9a works through the numbers.

**Why.** This round predicts most closely what you will do in the job. A strong candidate makes the tradeoffs explicit, while a weak one lists tools.

**How to answer well.** Spend the first five minutes fixing numbers: users, queries per day, the p95 target and whether it means first token or full answer, the data size, and compliance needs. Every later choice should point back to one of those numbers.

**Common mistakes.** No evaluation story. Treating the LLM as a box that is always right. Forgetting that tokens cost money on every request.

### ML and LLM fundamentals

**What interviewers probe.** Tokenisation, attention and the KV cache at the level of intuition, why fine-tuning differs from RAG, what embeddings capture, what temperature and top-p do, and how decoding works. AI labs go deeper (training dynamics, scaling) than application-focused startups.

**Why.** You do not need to derive backprop, but you must be able to explain failures. "Why does the model ignore the middle of a long prompt?" needs some model-level understanding (see 27.7a).

**How to answer well.** Answer each question at two levels: one sentence of intuition, then one sentence on what it changes in production. For example: "The KV cache stores the attention keys and values of earlier tokens so they are not recomputed. In production that means memory grows with context length times concurrency, which is why long-context traffic limits batch size." Chapter 4 ([LLM internals](/docs/genai-handbook/04-llm-internals)) is the revision source.

**Common mistakes.** Bluffing about maths you have not studied. A clean "I know the intuition, not the derivation" scores better than a confused derivation.

### Project deep-dive

**What interviewers probe.** One project from your resume, for 30 to 45 minutes. They will push on every decision: why that chunk size, why LangGraph rather than a plain loop, how you know it works, what broke in production, and what you would change.

**Why.** It is the hardest round to fake. The depth of your answers to "why" shows whether you did the work or watched it being done.

**How to answer well.** Prepare one architecture diagram you can draw from memory, three decisions with the alternatives you rejected, one incident, and your eval numbers (see 27.5a). Know your own costs, such as cost per conversation and p95 latency, without having to look them up.

**Common mistakes.** Choosing your most impressive-sounding project rather than the one you know best. Not being able to name a single thing that went wrong.

## 27.2 System Design Interviews

These are the most common interviews for GenAI engineers. You will be given a product ("Design a ChatGPT clone," "Design an enterprise RAG," "Design a multi-agent platform") and 45-60 minutes.

Approach:
1. **Clarify requirements** for 5 minutes. Functional, non-functional (latency, throughput, availability), constraints (budget, compliance, scale).
2. **Sketch high-level architecture.** Boxes and arrows.
3. **Dive into one or two components** the interviewer indicates.
4. **Discuss tradeoffs** at every choice.
5. **Address scaling, failure modes, cost.**

Common pitfalls:
- Jumping to specific tools before requirements.
- Skipping cost discussion.
- Single point of failure on the LLM provider.
- Ignoring evaluation and observability.
- Not addressing failure modes.

Practice with the eight designs in Chapter 22.

## 27.3 Coding Interviews

Coding interviews for GenAI engineers test fundamentals plus AI-specific patterns:

- **Async/await fluency.** Streaming endpoints, cancellation.
- **API design.** Request/response shapes, error handling, retries.
- **Data structures.** Vector similarity, ranking, deduplication.
- **Concurrency.** Worker pools, queues, backpressure.
- **Algorithms.** ANN intuition, BM25, hybrid scoring.

You will rarely be asked to implement attention from scratch. You will be asked to implement a small chat backend that streams tokens, implements rate limiting, and handles retries.

Prepare with two patterns: build a streaming API in your strongest language, and implement a simple retrieval+ranking pipeline.

## 27.3a Practice Problem: Hybrid Retrieval and Ranking in 40 Lines 🔧

This is the second practice pattern above written out, sized for a 45-minute live round.

**Problem.** "Given a query, a list of documents and their embeddings, return the top 5 documents using both keyword and vector relevance."

**Why interviewers like it.** It tests BM25 intuition, vector similarity, score fusion and clean TypeScript in one go, and it opens follow-ups on scaling.

**Mechanism.** BM25 scores exact term overlap and weights rare terms more heavily. Cosine similarity scores closeness in meaning. Their scores are on different scales, so you fuse the rankings rather than the raw scores. Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF) gives each document `1 / (k + rank)` from every list and adds the results together.

```ts
type Doc = { id: string; text: string; embedding: number[] };

const tokenize = (s: string) => s.toLowerCase().match(/[a-z0-9]+/g) ?? [];

function bm25Scores(query: string, docs: Doc[], k1 = 1.2, b = 0.75): Map<string, number> {
  const toks = docs.map((d) => tokenize(d.text));
  const N = docs.length;
  const avgdl = toks.reduce((s, t) => s + t.length, 0) / Math.max(N, 1);
  const df = new Map<string, number>();
  for (const t of toks) for (const term of new Set(t)) df.set(term, (df.get(term) ?? 0) + 1);

  const scores = new Map<string, number>();
  docs.forEach((doc, i) => {
    const tf = new Map<string, number>();
    for (const term of toks[i]) tf.set(term, (tf.get(term) ?? 0) + 1);
    let s = 0;
    for (const q of new Set(tokenize(query))) {
      const f = tf.get(q) ?? 0;
      if (f === 0) continue;
      const n = df.get(q)!;
      const idf = Math.log(1 + (N - n + 0.5) / (n + 0.5));
      s += (idf * f * (k1 + 1)) / (f + k1 * (1 - b + (b * toks[i].length) / avgdl));
    }
    scores.set(doc.id, s);
  });
  return scores;
}

function cosine(a: number[], b: number[]): number {
  let dot = 0, na = 0, nb = 0;
  for (let i = 0; i < a.length; i++) { dot += a[i] * b[i]; na += a[i] ** 2; nb += b[i] ** 2; }
  return na && nb ? dot / Math.sqrt(na * nb) : 0;
}

const rankIds = (scores: Map<string, number>) =>
  [...scores.entries()].sort((x, y) => y[1] - x[1]).map(([id]) => id);

export function hybridSearch(query: string, qEmb: number[], docs: Doc[], topK = 5, k = 60) {
  const lexical = rankIds(bm25Scores(query, docs));
  const semantic = rankIds(new Map(docs.map((d) => [d.id, cosine(qEmb, d.embedding)])));
  const fused = new Map<string, number>();
  for (const list of [lexical, semantic]) {
    list.forEach((id, rank) => fused.set(id, (fused.get(id) ?? 0) + 1 / (k + rank + 1)));
  }
  return rankIds(fused).slice(0, topK);
}
```

**Edge cases to raise without being asked.**
- **Zero-score documents.** BM25 gives 0 to documents with no overlap, but they still appear at the bottom of the lexical list and get a small RRF share. A stricter version drops zero scores before fusing.
- **Empty query or empty corpus.** `avgdl` guards against dividing by zero. Say so.
- **Scale.** This is a linear scan. In production the semantic list comes from an ANN index (HNSW) and the lexical list from an inverted index (Postgres full-text search, OpenSearch). You fuse the top 50 from each, not the whole corpus.
- **Why not add raw scores?** BM25 is unbounded and cosine lies between -1 and 1, so one signal would dominate. RRF needs no tuning, which is why it is the usual default.
- **The follow-up.** "How would you add a reranker?" Fuse to about 50 candidates, then score each (query, document) pair with a cross-encoder and keep the top 5. Chapter 10 ([RAG](/docs/genai-handbook/10-rag)) covers reranking in depth.

## 27.5a Talking About Evals and Metrics From Your Own Projects ➕

"Design an evaluation harness" is the abstract version of the question. The version that decides offers is "how do you know your system works?", asked about your own project.

### What interviewers probe

They want to know whether you had a fixed test set, what you measured, what the baseline was, how big the change was, and whether you trusted the metric. Follow-ups usually look for weak spots: "Who labelled the data?", "How did you validate the LLM judge?", "Did the metric match what users complained about?".

### Why it matters

LLM output is non-deterministic and hard to unit test, so teams that do not measure ship regressions without noticing. A candidate who has built even a small eval loop has shown the habit that matters most in this role.

### How to answer well: the five-part eval story

Structure the answer as dataset, metric, baseline, change, decision.

1. **Dataset.** "I built 120 test cases: 80 real user queries sampled from logs (with PII removed) and 40 hand-written hard cases such as multi-hop questions and questions with no answer in the corpus."
2. **Metric.** Pick one per layer. Retrieval: recall at 5 (did a correct chunk appear in the top 5?). Generation: faithfulness (is every claim supported by the retrieved context?), scored by an LLM judge. System: p95 time to first token and cost per query.
3. **Baseline.** "Pure vector search scored recall at 5 of 0.71."
4. **Change.** "Adding BM25 with RRF and a cross-encoder reranker raised it to 0.86. Faithfulness went from 3.9 to 4.4 out of 5. p95 latency rose by 180 ms because of the reranker."
5. **Decision.** "We kept the reranker, since the latency stayed inside budget, and made the eval suite a CI gate so any prompt or chunking change that dropped recall by more than 3 points failed the build."

The numbers above are a template. Use your own, and if you never measured something, say "I didn't measure that, but here's how I would." That is a much better answer than an invented figure, which a good interviewer will expose with a single follow-up question.

### Metrics for a voice agent project

If your strongest project is a LiveKit voice agent, the eval story uses different metrics:
- **Latency per turn**, split into end-of-utterance delay, LLM time to first token and TTS time to first byte (28.6a shows how to collect these).
- **False interruption rate**: how often the agent starts speaking while the user is still talking.
- **STT word error rate** on a recorded sample set, measured per accent or language if you serve several.
- **Task success rate**: did the call reach its goal (booking made, ticket raised) without a human taking over?

### Validating the judge

The follow-up that separates strong candidates is "how do you know the LLM judge is right?". A good answer: "I hand-labelled 50 outputs, ran the judge on the same 50 and measured agreement. It agreed about 85% of the time, and the disagreements were mostly partial answers, so I rewrote the rubric to define partial credit explicitly." Also mention known judge biases: a preference for longer answers, for the first option shown, and for outputs from its own model family.

### Common mistakes

- Quoting a single overall accuracy number with no dataset size.
- Using only LLM-as-judge with no human check.
- Measuring quality but not latency and cost, which are part of the same tradeoff.
- Evaluating on the same examples you used while tuning the prompt, so the score is inflated.

Section 28.4a has a runnable eval harness you can adapt for your own project.

## 27.7a Common Trick Questions ➕

Scenario questions test your process. A second group of questions sounds simple but has a common wrong answer, and interviewers use them to check whether you understand the mechanism or just the slogan.

### "If I set temperature to 0, is the output deterministic?"

**What they probe.** Whether you know the gap between greedy decoding in theory and serving systems in practice.

**Why it is a trick.** Temperature 0 means greedy decoding: always pick the most likely token. So the obvious answer is yes. In practice repeated identical requests can still return different text.

**Model answer.** "Not in practice. Greedy decoding is deterministic only if the logits are bit-for-bit identical on every run. Production servers batch your request with other users' requests, and the batch size changes from moment to moment. Many GPU kernels (matrix multiplies, attention, normalisation) split their reductions differently depending on batch size, and floating-point addition is not associative, so the logits differ slightly. When two tokens are nearly tied, a tiny difference flips the choice, and from that point the rest of the output diverges. Thinking Machines Lab showed in September 2025 that batch-invariant kernels make temperature-0 inference reproducible in vLLM, though in their tests it ran about 1.6 times slower than the default kernels. So for tests I do not rely on exact string matches. I use a seed where the provider supports one, compare outputs semantically or by schema, and use structured outputs for anything a program will parse."

**Common mistake.** Saying "set a seed and it's solved". Seeds control sampling randomness, not batching effects.

### "Why does RAG fail even when the answer is in the corpus?"

**What they probe.** Whether you can debug RAG layer by layer instead of blaming the model.

**Model answer.** Go through the pipeline in order, giving each layer a test you would run:
1. **Ingestion.** The answer sits in a table or scanned PDF that the loader dropped or garbled. Test: inspect the stored chunk text.
2. **Chunking.** The answer was split across two chunks, or the chunk lost its heading context ("it costs 40 dollars" with no product name). Test: find the chunk that should match and read it.
3. **Retrieval.** The query uses different words from the document (vocabulary mismatch), or it contains an exact identifier that embeddings handle badly. Test: recall at k on a labelled set. Fix with hybrid search or query rewriting.
4. **Ranking and truncation.** The right chunk was retrieved at rank 12 but only the top 5 were sent. Test: log ranks. Fix with a reranker.
5. **Generation.** The context was right but the model ignored it or mixed it with prior knowledge, or the relevant chunk sat in the middle of a long context, where models attend to information less reliably (the "lost in the middle" result from Liu et al.). Test: faithfulness eval.
6. **Access control and freshness.** The chunk exists but was filtered out by ACLs, or the index is stale.

**Common mistake.** Answering "use a bigger model" or "tune the prompt" before checking retrieval recall.

### "We have a 1M-token context window. Why do we still need memory or RAG?"

**What they probe.** Whether you separate what a model *can* read from what it *should* read on each call.

**Model answer.** "The context window is working memory for one call. It is not persistent, it is billed on every request, and it slows down time to first token because the whole prompt must be prefilled. Memory is a storage and retrieval system that decides what goes into that window. Even with a huge window I would still retrieve, because cost and latency grow with input length, quality degrades on information buried mid-context, and the data changes. You do not want to resend a million tokens because one document was edited. Prompt caching softens the cost of a stable prefix, but it does not remove the need to select. Long context does replace retrieval for bounded, one-off jobs, such as analysing one large contract."

**Common mistake.** Treating "memory" and "context" as synonyms, or claiming long context makes RAG obsolete.

### More ➕ quick-fire questions

- **"Does a lower temperature make the model more accurate?"** **No. It makes the model more consistent. If the most likely answer is wrong, temperature 0 gives you the wrong answer every time.**
- **"Why did my fine-tuned model forget how to follow instructions?"** **Catastrophic forgetting from narrow training data. Mix in general instruction data, use a lower learning rate or LoRA, and evaluate on a held-out general set.**
- **"Why is my streaming endpoint fast locally but slow in production?"** **Usually a buffering proxy or load balancer, or compression middleware, holding back SSE chunks. Disable buffering for that route and flush after each event.**
- **"Can a model count the letters in a word reliably?"** **Not reliably. It sees tokens, not characters. Give it a tool for exact operations.**

## 27.9 Sample System Design Walkthrough — Enterprise RAG

A condensed example.

**Clarifications.**
- 10k internal users, 50M documents from Confluence, SharePoint.
- p95 under 3 seconds.
- SSO via Okta.
- Audit log retained 7 years.
- Data residency: EU and US.

**HLD.**
- Browser → SSO → API gateway → Chat service.
- Chat service → Retrieval service (hybrid vector + BM25 + reranker) → LLM gateway → managed model.
- Audit log to immutable store.
- Per-region deployment.

**LLD.**
- Chunks tagged with source, ACL groups, language, timestamp.
- Vector DB sharded by tenant for isolation.
- Reranker: Cohere or BGE-Reranker.
- Citations rendered as clickable references.

**Tradeoffs.**
- Hybrid retrieval over pure vector for recall.
- Managed model (Bedrock or Azure OpenAI) over self-hosted because the volume does not justify GPU ops.
- Per-region indices for residency.

**Scale.** Linear in users; document growth handled by reshard.

**Failure modes.** Provider outage → secondary provider. Vector DB partition → degraded mode (keyword only).

**Cost.** Roughly $X per query × Y queries/day = $Z/month.

This is the shape of a strong answer.

## 27.9a Worked Budget for the Enterprise RAG Design 🔧

The walkthrough above ends with "$X per query × Y queries/day". This section fills in those numbers, because interviewers increasingly ask you to do the arithmetic live.

### Step 1: pin down what "p95 under 3 seconds" means

**Problem.** A 400-token answer streamed at around 80 tokens per second takes about 5 seconds to generate on its own, so "full answer in 3 seconds" cannot be met with a typical model.

**Why it matters.** Catching this early is a strong signal. Users perceive time to first token (TTFT), because the answer appears as it streams.

**Resolution.** Propose: "p95 TTFT under 1.5 s, full answer streaming, p95 completion under 8 s." Then confirm it with the interviewer.

### Step 2: latency budget for time to first token

<Mermaid chart={`sequenceDiagram
  participant U as Browser
  participant G as API gateway
  participant C as Chat service
  participant R as Retrieval service
  participant L as LLM gateway and model
  U->>G: POST /chat (SSO token)
  G->>C: auth and rate limit (about 30 ms)
  C->>R: embed query (about 40 ms)
  R->>R: hybrid search, vector and BM25 (about 120 ms)
  R->>R: rerank 50 candidates (about 150 ms)
  R-->>C: top 6 chunks with ACL filter applied
  C->>L: prompt about 3,500 tokens
  L-->>C: first token (TTFT about 600 to 900 ms)
  C-->>U: SSE stream begins (total about 1.0 to 1.3 s)`} />

| Stage | Budget (p95) | Lever if over budget |
|---|---|---|
| Gateway, auth, rate limit | 30 ms | Cache JWKS keys, keep Redis in the same region |
| Query embedding | 40 ms | Small embedding model, same region |
| Hybrid retrieval | 120 ms | HNSW tuning, ACL pre-filter |
| Rerank 50 candidates | 150 ms | Fewer candidates, smaller cross-encoder |
| LLM TTFT | 900 ms | Shorter prompt, prompt caching, faster tier |
| Headroom | 260 ms | Network jitter, retries |
| **Total TTFT** | **1.5 s** | |

These are planning numbers to state as assumptions, not vendor measurements. Say "I would measure each one in a load test before committing."

### Step 3: cost per query and per month

State the assumptions out loud so the interviewer can correct them:
- 10,000 users and 10 queries per user per day, so 100,000 queries per day.
- Input per query: 500 tokens of system prompt and history plus 6 chunks of 500 tokens, about 3,500 tokens. Output about 400 tokens.
- **Placeholder prices**: $1 per million input tokens and $5 per million output tokens. Use the provider's current price sheet in a real design.

```ts
type Assumptions = {
  queriesPerDay: number;
  inputTokens: number;
  outputTokens: number;
  inPricePerM: number;   // USD per 1M input tokens
  outPricePerM: number;  // USD per 1M output tokens
  cacheHitRate: number;  // share of queries answered from a semantic cache
};

export function llmCost(a: Assumptions) {
  const perQuery = (a.inputTokens * a.inPricePerM + a.outputTokens * a.outPricePerM) / 1e6;
  const billedQueries = a.queriesPerDay * (1 - a.cacheHitRate);
  const perDay = perQuery * billedQueries;
  return { perQuery, perDay, perMonth: perDay * 30 };
}

console.log(llmCost({
  queriesPerDay: 100_000, inputTokens: 3_500, outputTokens: 400,
  inPricePerM: 1, outPricePerM: 5, cacheHitRate: 0,
}));
// perQuery = (3500 * 1 + 400 * 5) / 1e6 = 0.0055 USD
// perDay   = 550 USD, perMonth = 16,500 USD
```

**What to say next.** Input is 64% of the cost (3,500 of 5,500 micro-dollars), so the levers are on the input side: fewer or shorter chunks, provider prompt caching for the fixed system prompt, and a semantic cache for repeated questions. A 20% cache hit rate cuts the bill to about $13,200 per month. Then add the fixed costs: vector DB, reranker hosting and observability. This is also where you justify "managed model over self-hosted": at about 390 million tokens a day (roughly 12 billion a month) you are above the volume where Chapter 25 ([cost optimization](/docs/genai-handbook/25-cost-optimization)) says self-hosting a small model wins on unit cost. This design needs a stronger model for answer quality, though, and the GPU operations staff, multi-region capacity and compliance work usually tip the balance back to managed for an internal tool. Say that you would revisit the decision once volume or a routing split makes a small self-hosted model viable for part of the traffic.

**Edge cases.** Peak load is not average load. Internal tools peak during working hours, so size rate limits and provider quotas for about 3 times the average per second. Reranker GPU cost does not fall with caching unless you cache before retrieval.

## 27.10 Sample Coding Question — Streaming Endpoint

"Sketch the request flow for a streaming chat endpoint with per-user rate limit and cancellation."

The strong answer (described in prose, since the user prefers text):

The endpoint receives a POST with the message. The handler authenticates via JWT, checks the rate limit via Redis token bucket (rejecting with 429 if exceeded), opens a server-sent event response stream, and begins streaming from the LLM client. Each token received from the LLM is forwarded to the client. The handler observes client disconnection (via the request being cancelled by the framework) and propagates cancellation to the LLM client, freeing GPU time. On completion, it logs usage to the observability pipeline and increments the user's token counter.

Trade-offs to mention: SSE vs WebSocket; backpressure handling if the client is slow; how cancellation is implemented in the chosen async runtime; what happens if the LLM call fails mid-stream.

## 27.10a The Streaming Endpoint in Code 🔧

This is the prose answer above as runnable Node code, so you can practise typing it under time pressure.

**Mechanism in one line.** An atomic Redis token bucket decides whether to admit the request. An `AbortController` ties the client connection to the upstream LLM call, and `drain` events apply backpressure when the client reads slowly.

```ts
import express from "express";
import { once } from "node:events";
import Redis from "ioredis";
import OpenAI from "openai";

const app = express();
app.use(express.json());
const redis = new Redis(process.env.REDIS_URL!);
const openai = new OpenAI();

// Atomic token bucket. Uses Redis server time so app servers with skewed clocks agree.
const TAKE_TOKEN = `
local t = redis.call('TIME')
local now = tonumber(t[1]) * 1000 + math.floor(tonumber(t[2]) / 1000)
local cap = tonumber(ARGV[1])
local rate = tonumber(ARGV[2])            -- tokens per second
local b = redis.call('HMGET', KEYS[1], 'tokens', 'ts')
local tokens = tonumber(b[1]) or cap
local ts = tonumber(b[2]) or now
tokens = math.min(cap, tokens + (now - ts) / 1000 * rate)
local allowed = 0
if tokens >= 1 then tokens = tokens - 1; allowed = 1 end
redis.call('HSET', KEYS[1], 'tokens', tokens, 'ts', now)
redis.call('PEXPIRE', KEYS[1], math.ceil(cap / rate * 1000))
return allowed`;

// Assume an auth middleware has verified the JWT and set res.locals.userId.
app.post("/chat", async (req, res) => {
  const userId = res.locals.userId as string;
  const allowed = await redis.eval(TAKE_TOKEN, 1, `rl:${userId}`, 10, 0.5); // burst 10, 1 per 2 s
  if (allowed !== 1) return res.status(429).json({ error: "rate_limited" });

  res.writeHead(200, {
    "Content-Type": "text/event-stream",
    "Cache-Control": "no-cache",
    Connection: "keep-alive",
    "X-Accel-Buffering": "no", // stop nginx from buffering the stream
  });

  const ac = new AbortController();
  res.on("close", () => { if (!res.writableEnded) ac.abort(); }); // client went away

  let totalTokens = 0;
  try {
    const stream = await openai.chat.completions.create(
      {
        model: "gpt-4o-mini",
        messages: req.body.messages,
        stream: true,
        stream_options: { include_usage: true },
      },
      { signal: ac.signal },
    );
    for await (const chunk of stream) {
      const delta = chunk.choices[0]?.delta?.content;
      if (delta && !res.write(`data: ${JSON.stringify({ delta })}\n\n`)) {
        await once(res, "drain", { signal: ac.signal }); // backpressure
      }
      if (chunk.usage) totalTokens = chunk.usage.total_tokens;
    }
    res.write("event: done\ndata: {}\n\n");
  } catch (err) {
    if (!ac.signal.aborted) {
      // Headers are already sent, so report the failure in-band, not as a 500.
      res.write(`event: error\ndata: ${JSON.stringify({ code: "upstream_failed" })}\n\n`);
    }
  } finally {
    res.end();
    if (totalTokens) await redis.incrby(`usage:${userId}:${new Date().toISOString().slice(0, 10)}`, totalTokens);
  }
});

app.listen(3000);
```

**Edge cases interviewers push on.**
- **Failure mid-stream.** You cannot change the HTTP status after the first byte, so errors travel as an SSE `error` event. The client should keep the partial text and offer a retry. A blind automatic retry would show the user a second, different answer.
- **Why `res.on("close")` rather than `req.on("close")`.** In recent Node versions the request's `close` event can fire as soon as the body has been read, which would cancel healthy streams. The response's `close` event, combined with the `writableEnded` check, reliably means the client disconnected.
- **Usage when cancelled.** If the client aborts, the final usage chunk never arrives. Count tokens from the deltas, or estimate them, so cancelled requests are still billed to the right user.
- **Rate limiting by tokens, not requests.** A bucket per request lets one user send ten 100k-token prompts. A second bucket debited by the estimated input tokens closes that gap.
- **SSE vs WebSocket.** SSE is enough for one-way token streams and works through most proxies. Switch to WebSockets (or WebRTC for audio) when the client must send data mid-stream, as in voice barge-in.
- **The model name.** It is only an example. Read it from config so a model change does not need a deploy.

## 27.10b Take-Home Assignment Strategy ➕

Many AI startups replace live coding with a take-home, such as "build a small RAG service over these PDFs" or "build an agent that books meetings using this mock API". It is scored differently from a live round.

### What interviewers probe

Judgement more than volume. The usual rubric looks at whether it runs on the first try, whether the scope is sensible, whether you measured the result, how readable the code is, and whether the README explains the tradeoffs. Reviewers typically spend 20 to 40 minutes on a submission, so anything they cannot find quickly does not count.

### Why it matters

The take-home is the only round where you control the whole result. It shows how you would work on the job: what you choose to build, what you leave out, and how you communicate it.

### How to do it well

1. **Ask before you start.** "Is it fine to use a hosted model API?", "Which matters more, retrieval quality or code structure?" and "Is the time limit a guideline or strict?". Questions like these signal seniority.
2. **Respect the time box.** If they say 4 hours, deliver 4 to 6 hours of well-finished work, not 20 hours of features. List what you would do next instead of building it.
3. **Make it run with one command.** `docker compose up`, or `npm install && npm run dev` with a `.env.example` file. Test on a clean clone before submitting.
4. **Ship a tiny eval.** Even 15 test questions with a recall or pass-rate number puts you ahead of most submissions. "Recall at 5 is 0.80 on 15 questions, and here are the 3 failures and why" is a strong line to put in a README.
5. **Write the README as a design document.** Cover the architecture (a small diagram), key decisions with the alternatives you rejected, the eval results, known limitations, and what you would do with another week.
6. **Handle the obvious failure paths.** Timeouts on LLM calls, malformed model JSON (validate with Zod and retry once), an empty corpus and missing API keys.

A README outline that reviewers can scan quickly:

```text
# <Project name>
Run:            docker compose up   (needs OPENAI_API_KEY in .env)
Architecture:   one diagram + 5 lines
Decisions:      chunking 800 tokens / 100 overlap (tried 400: lost table context)
                hybrid BM25 + vector with RRF (pure vector missed product codes)
Evaluation:     15 questions, recall@5 = 0.80, faithfulness 4.3/5 (judge prompt in /evals)
Limitations:    no auth, single tenant, PDFs with scanned images not OCR'd
Next steps:     reranker, streaming UI, per-tenant indexes
Time spent:     about 5 hours
```

### Common mistakes

- Over-engineering: Kubernetes manifests for a 4-hour exercise.
- No tests or eval of any kind.
- Committing API keys. Reviewers notice, and some reject the submission for it alone.
- A README that only says "npm start".
- Not being able to explain your own code in the follow-up review. If an AI coding assistant wrote parts of it, make sure you understand every line and say how you used the tool if asked.

## 27.11 Sample Behavioral Answer Pattern

"Tell me about a tradeoff between speed and quality."

"In a previous project, we shipped a feature that used a frontier model for every query. Quality was excellent but cost ran 3× our forecast. I led an analysis that showed 70% of queries were easily handled by a smaller model. We implemented a router based on input length and detected intent. Quality dropped on 5% of queries; cost dropped 60%. We built an offline replay to identify the regression cases and added them to our eval set. The lesson I took away: optimize after measuring; routing without data degrades quality. I now run a quarterly cost-vs-quality review on every AI feature I own."

Specific, honest, ends with a current practice.

## 27.11a STAR Stories for AI Work ➕

The answer above follows a structure without naming it. Naming it, and preparing several stories in that shape before the interview, is what makes the behavioural round predictable.

### What interviewers probe

Ownership, judgement under uncertainty, how you handle failure and disagreement, and whether your results were measured. In AI work they also probe one specific thing: whether you can resist hype. Have you ever argued *against* using an LLM, or rolled one back?

### Why STAR works

STAR stands for Situation, Task, Action, Result. Interviewers often score behavioural answers against a rubric with separate boxes for context, personal contribution and impact. STAR fills each box in order, which keeps you from spending three minutes on background and twenty seconds on what you did. A useful split is about 15% situation and task, 60% action, and 25% result and lesson.

### Build a story bank of six

Prepare one story for each of these prompts. Most behavioural questions map onto one of them.

| Prompt | AI-specific angle |
|---|---|
| A failure | A model or feature that underperformed in production |
| A tradeoff | Quality vs latency or cost |
| A disagreement | Pushing back on "just add GPT to it" |
| Ambiguity | Requirements with no clear definition of correct output |
| Going deep technically | A latency or reliability problem you diagnosed |
| Influence without authority | Getting a team to adopt evals or a gateway |

### A worked model answer

**Question:** "Tell me about a hard technical problem you solved."

> **Situation.** Our LiveKit voice agent for appointment booking had good transcription accuracy, but in user tests people said it felt rude. It talked over them and then paused awkwardly.
>
> **Task.** I owned the agent pipeline, and the goal was to make conversations feel natural without adding latency.
>
> **Action.** First I measured. I logged end-of-utterance delay, LLM time to first token and TTS time to first byte for every turn, plus how often the agent started speaking while the user was still talking. The data showed two separate problems. Silence-based endpointing fired during natural pauses such as reading out a phone number, which caused the interruptions. And the long system prompt made TTFT the largest share of the delay. I added a semantic turn-detection model on top of VAD, so a pause after "my number is nine eight four" no longer counted as the end of a turn. I also trimmed the system prompt and moved static instructions into a cached prefix. Finally, I built a set of 40 recorded test conversations so we could rerun the measurements after each change.
>
> **Result.** False interruptions fell sharply on the test set, and median response latency dropped by a few hundred milliseconds. Booking completion in the next pilot went up. The lesson I took is that voice quality is a latency-distribution problem, not a model problem, so now I instrument every pipeline stage before I tune anything.

Use your own real numbers in place of the qualitative phrases. A precise number ("from 18% to 6% of turns") is the most persuasive part of a STAR answer.

### Common mistakes

- **"We" throughout.** Say "I" for your actions and "we" for team outcomes.
- **No result, or a result with no number.**
- **A failure story where nothing really failed.** "I worked too hard" is not a failure story. Choose a real one and focus on what you changed afterwards.
- **One story stretched to fit every question.** Interviewers compare notes, and repetition reads as thin experience.
- **Blaming the model.** "The LLM hallucinated" is a situation, not a root cause. Explain what in the system let the hallucination reach a user.

## 27.13 Company-Specific Notes

- **FAANG.** Strong system design and coding; full loops including data structures.

## 27.15 Salary and Leveling

GenAI engineers command premium compensation in 2025-2026 due to scarcity. Expect:
- Mid (3-5 years): top-of-band software engineer compensation.
- Senior (5-10 years): senior+ software engineer, often with equity premium.
- Staff (10+ years): staff/principal level with significant equity.

Leveling differs by company. Read the job description carefully; "ML Engineer" at one company is "Software Engineer" at another.
