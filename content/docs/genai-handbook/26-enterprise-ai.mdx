## 26.1 Concept Explanation

Enterprise AI is the deployment of GenAI inside large organizations with the constraints that come with size: governance, compliance, audit, multi-tenancy, integration with existing systems, risk management. The technical patterns are not fundamentally different from startup AI. The discipline is.

A startup ships fast; an enterprise ships *safely*. The architecture must accommodate legal, security, procurement, change management, and procurement-driven vendor selection. The deployment must satisfy regulators. The operations must produce audit evidence at any moment.

## 26.2 Governance

AI governance is the system of policies, processes, and oversight that ensures AI use aligns with the organization's risk posture and regulatory obligations.

Components:
- **AI Council or Committee.** Cross-functional body (legal, security, engineering, business) that approves AI use cases.
- **Use case inventory.** Every AI feature registered, with owner, purpose, data inputs, risk class.
- **Risk classification.** Tiered (high-risk, medium-risk, low-risk) with different review depths.
- **Acceptable use policy.** What is and is not allowed.
- **Vendor evaluation.** Documented process for selecting AI vendors.

Without governance, every team ships AI independently, and compliance gaps appear at audit time.

## 26.3 Compliance

Compliance varies by industry. The most common frameworks for AI:

**SOC 2.** Service Organization Control 2. Information security controls audited annually. Most B2B SaaS requires it.

**GDPR.** EU data protection. Affects any system processing EU resident data. Lawful basis, right to deletion, data residency, transparency obligations all apply to AI.

**HIPAA.** US healthcare. Patient health information must be protected with specific controls. AI vendors must sign BAAs.

**PCI DSS.** Payment card data. AI features touching card data require segmented infrastructure.

**EU AI Act.** Tiered regulation by AI risk class. High-risk AI (employment, credit, education) faces strict obligations. Even general-purpose models face transparency obligations.

**FedRAMP.** US federal compliance. AI vendors selling to federal customers must clear high security bars.

**ISO 27001 / 27701 / 42001.** International standards for information security and AI management.

Compliance is not a checkbox. It shapes architecture: data residency forces regional deployment, retention policies force deletion pipelines, audit requirements force pervasive logging.

## 26.3a Three Frameworks at an Engineering Level 🔧

The list above names the frameworks; for three of them (EU AI Act, SOC 2, ISO/IEC 42001) this section turns the obligations into the artefacts and code an engineering team actually produces.

### EU AI Act: your role decides your obligations

**Problem.** Teams read "high-risk AI faces strict obligations" and either panic or ignore it. Both are expensive.

**Why it matters.** The Act assigns duties by role. A **provider** develops an AI system (or has one developed) and places it on the market under its own name. A **deployer** uses one under its own authority. If you build an HR screening product on top of a third-party LLM, you are the provider of the screening system, even though someone else made the model. If you use a vendor's screening tool internally, you are a deployer. Fines reach up to €35 million or 7% of worldwide turnover for prohibited practices, and up to €15 million or 3% for most other breaches.

**Timeline as of October 2026.** The Act entered into force on 1 August 2024. Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 (the "Digital Omnibus on AI", in force since 27 July 2026) moved the high-risk dates:

| Date | What applies |
|---|---|
| 2 February 2025 | Prohibited practices and the AI literacy provision |
| 2 August 2025 | Obligations for general-purpose AI model providers |
| 2 August 2026 | Article 50 transparency duties (tell people they are talking to an AI, label deepfakes) for new systems |
| 2 December 2026 | Machine-readable marking of AI-generated content for systems already on the market before 2 August 2026, and two new prohibitions (non-consensual intimate imagery, child sexual abuse material) |
| 2 December 2027 | Stand-alone high-risk systems in Annex III (employment, education, credit and insurance, essential services, law enforcement, migration, justice, critical infrastructure, biometrics) |
| 2 August 2028 | High-risk AI that is a safety component of products covered by Annex I (medical devices, machinery, toys and similar) |

**Mechanism: obligations mapped to artefacts.** For a high-risk system, the provider duties translate into concrete engineering work:

| Article | Duty | Engineering artefact |
|---|---|---|
| 9 | Risk management system | Risk register per use case, reviewed each release |
| 10 | Data governance | Dataset documentation, bias checks on eval and training data |
| 11 | Technical documentation | Architecture, model versions, eval results, kept current |
| 12 | Record-keeping | Automatic event logs over the system's lifetime (see 26.4a) |
| 13 | Transparency to deployers | Instructions for use, known limitations, accuracy figures |
| 14 | Human oversight | Review and override workflow (see 26.6a) |
| 15 | Accuracy, robustness, cybersecurity | Eval gates, red-team results, prompt-injection defences |
| 72 and 73 | Post-market monitoring, serious incident reporting | Production quality monitoring, incident runbook with regulator notification |

Deployers of high-risk systems must use them according to the instructions, assign competent people to oversee them, monitor them, and keep the logs the system generates for at least six months (Article 26). Some deployers, including public bodies and those doing credit scoring or life and health insurance pricing, must also run a fundamental rights impact assessment (Article 27).

### Implementation: risk classification as a deploy gate

```python
# A first-pass screen, run in CI against the use-case register. Legal review makes the final call,
# including the Article 6(3) exceptions for systems that only do narrow preparatory tasks.
from typing import Literal

from pydantic import BaseModel

ANNEX_III_AREAS = frozenset({
    "biometrics", "critical_infrastructure", "education", "employment",
    "essential_services", "law_enforcement", "migration", "justice", "democratic_processes",
})

type Artefact = Literal["risk_register", "tech_doc", "human_oversight", "log_retention_6m", "fria"]


class UseCase(BaseModel):
    id: str
    owner: str
    areas: list[str]                   # business domains the system touches
    decides_about_people: bool         # outputs affect access to jobs, credit, services...
    user_facing_chatbot: bool
    generates_synthetic_media: bool
    artefacts: dict[Artefact, str] = {}  # artefact -> link to the document


class Screening(BaseModel):
    id: str
    tier: Literal["high", "limited-or-minimal"]
    missing: list[Artefact]
    transparency_duties: list[str]
    block: bool


def screen(u: UseCase) -> Screening:
    high_risk = u.decides_about_people and any(a in ANNEX_III_AREAS for a in u.areas)
    required: list[Artefact] = (
        ["risk_register", "tech_doc", "human_oversight", "log_retention_6m"] if high_risk else []
    )
    missing = [k for k in required if not u.artefacts.get(k)]
    transparency_duties = [
        *(["disclose AI interaction"] if u.user_facing_chatbot else []),
        *(["machine-readable marking of AI output"] if u.generates_synthetic_media else []),
    ]
    return Screening(
        id=u.id,
        tier="high" if high_risk else "limited-or-minimal",
        missing=missing,
        transparency_duties=transparency_duties,
        block=bool(missing),
    )
```

**Edge cases.** A general-purpose chatbot becomes high-risk when a customer uses it to rank job applicants; your contract and instructions for use should forbid uses you have not assessed. A substantial modification (for example swapping the model behind a high-risk system) can require a fresh conformity assessment, so route model changes on high-risk systems through change management (26.10).

### SOC 2: what auditors ask about AI features

**Problem.** SOC 2 has no AI section, so teams assume their existing report covers the new feature. Then the auditor asks how a prompt change reached production.

**Mechanism.** SOC 2 reports on the AICPA Trust Services Criteria. A Type I report covers the design of controls at a point in time; a Type II covers whether they operated over a period, usually 6 to 12 months. The common criteria that AI features touch:

- **Change management (CC8).** Prompts, model IDs, retrieval configs and tool definitions are production changes. They need review, approval and a record, just like code. Keeping them in the repo with the eval gate from [24.10a](/docs/genai-handbook/24-production-failures#2410a-pinning-models-and-gating-upgrades-on-evals-) produces that evidence for free.
- **Logical access (CC6).** Who can read prompt logs (which contain customer data), who can change the system prompt, who holds provider API keys.
- **Monitoring (CC7).** Alerts on abuse, cost anomalies and quality, and evidence that someone responds.
- **Vendor management (CC9.2).** The LLM provider is a subservice organisation: collect its SOC 2 report each year and review the complementary controls it expects from you.

**Edge case.** Evidence must cover the whole audit period. A logging pipeline that was off for two weeks shows up as an exception in a Type II report.

### ISO/IEC 42001: a management system, not a checklist

**Problem.** Customers start asking for ISO/IEC 42001 certification and engineering treats it like a security scan.

**Mechanism.** ISO/IEC 42001:2023 (published December 2023) is the first certifiable standard for an AI management system. Like ISO 27001, its clauses 4 to 10 require a management cycle: scope, leadership, risk planning, resources, operation, performance evaluation, improvement. Annex A lists 38 reference controls under 9 objectives (A.2 to A.10), covering AI policy, roles, resources, impact assessment, the AI system life cycle, data, information for interested parties, responsible use and third parties. You pick the controls that apply and justify every exclusion in a Statement of Applicability.

The engineering artefacts that carry most of the weight:

1. An **AI system impact assessment** per system (who could be harmed and how), updated on major changes.
2. **Life-cycle records**: requirements, design, verification (evals), deployment and monitoring for each AI system.
3. **Data records**: where training, fine-tuning, retrieval and eval data came from, and its quality checks.
4. **Supplier controls** for model providers.

**Edge case.** Certification is audited every year (surveillance audits) within a three-year cycle. An inventory that was accurate for the first audit and never updated fails the next one, so generate it from the same register the CI gate above reads.

## 26.4 Audit Logging

Audit logs answer: who did what, when, why. For AI:
- Every model call (user, model, prompt hash, output hash, cost).
- Every tool invocation.
- Every retrieval (which docs, by whom).
- Every prompt or model change.
- Every secret access.
- Every admin action.

Logs are immutable, retained per policy (often 1-7 years for regulated industries), reviewed periodically. The discipline is logging early and reliably, not after a regulator asks.

## 26.4a A Tamper-Evident Audit Trail of Prompts and Outputs 🔧

"Logs are immutable" is easy to write and hard to prove; this section shows how to make tampering detectable and how to square immutable logs with deletion rights.

### Hashes or full content?

**Problem.** The handbook lists "prompt hash, output hash" per call. A hash proves that a prompt has not changed, but it cannot show an auditor or a customer what the model was actually asked. Storing full content proves more but turns the audit log into a store of personal data.

**Why (the numbers).** At 1 million model calls a day with about 8 KB of prompt plus output each, full content is about 8 GB a day, roughly 2.9 TB a year, and about 20 TB over a 7-year retention period, before replicas. That is affordable in object storage but needs tiering, access control and a deletion story.

**Mechanism.** Split the record in two:

1. **Audit event (small, kept for the full period).** Who, what, when, which model and prompt version, cost, the hash of the content, and a hash link to the previous event.
2. **Content (large, encrypted, shorter retention).** The full prompt and output, encrypted with a per-data-subject (or per-tenant) key, stored in object storage with write-once retention.

The hash chain makes edits detectable: each event's hash covers the previous event's hash, so changing or deleting any row breaks every hash after it. Periodically copying the latest hash to write-once storage (S3 Object Lock, for example) means even a database administrator cannot rewrite the chain without the mismatch showing.

### Implementation

```sql
CREATE TABLE audit_events (
  id           bigserial PRIMARY KEY,
  tenant_id    uuid NOT NULL,
  actor        text NOT NULL,          -- user ID, service account or agent run ID
  action       text NOT NULL,          -- 'model_call', 'tool_call', 'retrieval', 'prompt_change', ...
  details      jsonb NOT NULL,         -- model, prompt version, doc IDs, cost; no raw content
  content_hash text,                   -- sha256 of the encrypted content blob, if any
  content_ref  text,                   -- object storage key of that blob
  prev_hash    text NOT NULL,
  hash         text NOT NULL,
  created_at   timestamptz NOT NULL DEFAULT now()
);
-- The application role may insert and read, never update or delete
REVOKE UPDATE, DELETE ON audit_events FROM app_role;
```

```python
import datetime as dt
import hashlib
from dataclasses import asdict, dataclass, field
from typing import Any

from sqlalchemy import bindparam, text
from sqlalchemy.dialects.postgresql import JSONB
from sqlalchemy.ext.asyncio import AsyncEngine


@dataclass(frozen=True, slots=True)
class AuditInput:
    tenant_id: str
    actor: str
    action: str
    details: dict[str, Any] = field(default_factory=dict)
    content_hash: str | None = None
    content_ref: str | None = None


GENESIS = "0" * 64


def sha256(s: str) -> str:
    return hashlib.sha256(s.encode()).hexdigest()


def event_hash(prev_hash: str, e: AuditInput, created_at: dt.datetime) -> str:
    # stable_json sorts keys (see 24.13a), so the hash does not depend on key order
    return sha256(prev_hash + stable_json({**asdict(e), "created_at": created_at.isoformat()}))


INSERT_EVENT = text("""
    INSERT INTO audit_events (tenant_id, actor, action, details, content_hash, content_ref, prev_hash, hash, created_at)
    VALUES (:tenant_id, :actor, :action, :details, :content_hash, :content_ref, :prev_hash, :hash, :created_at)
""").bindparams(bindparam("details", type_=JSONB))


async def append_audit(engine: AsyncEngine, e: AuditInput) -> None:
    async with engine.begin() as conn:  # COMMIT on success, ROLLBACK on any exception
        # One chain per tenant; the advisory lock serialises appends within a tenant
        await conn.execute(text("SELECT pg_advisory_xact_lock(hashtext(:t))"), {"t": e.tenant_id})
        prev_hash = await conn.scalar(
            text("SELECT hash FROM audit_events WHERE tenant_id = :t ORDER BY id DESC LIMIT 1"),
            {"t": e.tenant_id},
        ) or GENESIS
        created_at = dt.datetime.now(dt.UTC)
        await conn.execute(INSERT_EVENT, {
            **asdict(e),
            "prev_hash": prev_hash,
            "hash": event_hash(prev_hash, e, created_at),
            "created_at": created_at,
        })
```

Verification walks the chain in `id` order and recomputes each hash; the first mismatch marks where tampering (or a bug) happened. Run it daily and alert on failure.

### Edge cases and failure modes

- **Right to erasure versus immutability.** Do not delete audit rows. Store personal content only in the encrypted blobs, and delete the data subject's key when an erasure request is approved ("crypto-shredding"). The audit event stays valid, the content becomes unreadable. Agree this approach with your privacy team, and keep personal data out of `details`.
- **Hash ordering bugs.** If the hash covers a timestamp generated by the database but the app hashes its own timestamp, verification fails. Hash exactly what you store, as the code above does.
- **The audit write fails.** For regulated actions, fail the action if the audit write fails ("no log, no action"). For low-risk reads, buffer to a durable queue instead of blocking users.
- **Volume.** A single chain per tenant serialises writes for that tenant. For very busy tenants, shard the chain (for example per tenant per hour) and chain the shard heads together.
- **Access to the logs is itself audited.** Reading prompt logs is a sensitive action. Log who searched the audit log and why.

## 26.5 Policy Enforcement

Policies become real when enforced in code:
- **Content policies** enforced by classifiers on inputs and outputs.
- **Data classification** policies enforced by detectors before logging or training.
- **Access policies** enforced by RBAC/ABAC at every layer.
- **Tool use policies** enforced by gateways that scope tool calls.
- **Model use policies** enforced by routing — frontier models gated to high-trust use cases.

A policy engine (OPA, Cedar, Cerbos) lets policies live as code, separate from application logic, versioned and reviewed.

## 26.6 Enterprise Onboarding

Bringing a new AI feature into enterprise deployment is a process:

1. Use case proposal with risk assessment.
2. Security review.
3. Legal review (data, IP, vendor terms).
4. Pilot in a controlled environment.
5. Evaluation against acceptance criteria.
6. Production deployment with phased rollout.
7. Ongoing monitoring and review.

Faster startups skip these steps and ship; enterprises follow them and ship reliably. The latter scales better in regulated environments.

## 26.6a Approval Workflows and Human Review at Runtime ➕

The onboarding steps above approve a feature once; many enterprise AI features also need a person to approve individual outputs or actions while the system runs, which the chapter does not cover.

### Where runtime review is needed and why it breaks

**Problem.** An agent can draft a refund, a contract clause or a credit decision. Policy (and, for high-risk systems, Article 14 of the EU AI Act) says a person must be able to review and override it. Teams add an "approve" button and call it done.

**Why it breaks (the numbers).** Review capacity is finite. 3,000 actions a day at 45 seconds each is 37.5 hours of review a day, about five full-time reviewers. When the queue grows, reviewers speed up and approve almost everything ("automation bias"). An approval rate near 100% is a sign the review has become a formality, not a sign the model is perfect.

### The mechanism

1. **Risk-based routing.** Auto-approve low-risk actions under clear limits (refunds under $50, internal drafts), require review above them, and require two reviewers for the highest tier.
2. **A state machine with expiry.** Every proposed action is stored with its full context and goes through explicit states. Pending actions expire, so stale approvals cannot be executed days later on changed data.
3. **Separation of duties.** The person who triggered the request cannot approve it, and approvers need a specific role.
4. **Measure the reviewers.** Seed the queue with a small share of known-bad actions and track how many reviewers catch.

### Implementation

```sql
CREATE TABLE pending_actions (
  id               uuid PRIMARY KEY DEFAULT gen_random_uuid(),
  tenant_id        uuid NOT NULL,
  requested_by     text NOT NULL,
  kind             text NOT NULL,
  payload          jsonb NOT NULL,
  risk             text NOT NULL CHECK (risk IN ('low', 'medium', 'high')),

                                                   'expired', 'executed', 'failed')),
  approvals_needed int  NOT NULL,
  approved_by      text[] NOT NULL DEFAULT '{}',
```

```python
import json
from dataclasses import dataclass
from typing import Any, Literal

from sqlalchemy import text
from sqlalchemy.ext.asyncio import AsyncEngine

type Risk = Literal["low", "medium", "high"]


@dataclass(frozen=True, slots=True)
class ProposedAction:
    tenant_id: str
    requested_by: str
    kind: str
    payload: Any
    amount_usd: float = 0.0


@dataclass(frozen=True, slots=True)
class Approver:
    id: str
    roles: frozenset[str]


class ApprovalError(Exception):
    pass


def risk_of(a: ProposedAction) -> Risk:
    if a.kind == "refund" and a.amount_usd <= 50:
        return "low"
    if a.kind == "refund" and a.amount_usd <= 1_000:
        return "medium"
    return "high"


async def propose(engine: AsyncEngine, a: ProposedAction) -> dict[str, Any]:
    risk = risk_of(a)
    status = "auto_approved" if risk == "low" else "pending"
    async with engine.begin() as conn:
        row = (await conn.execute(
            text("""
                INSERT INTO pending_actions
                    (tenant_id, requested_by, kind, payload, risk, status, approvals_needed, expires_at)
                VALUES (:tenant_id, :requested_by, :kind, CAST(:payload AS jsonb), :risk, :status, :needed,
                        now() + interval '24 hours')
                RETURNING id, status
            """),
            {
                "tenant_id": a.tenant_id, "requested_by": a.requested_by, "kind": a.kind,
                "payload": json.dumps(a.payload), "risk": risk, "status": status,
                "needed": 2 if risk == "high" else 1,
            },
        )).one()
    return dict(row._mapping)  # tell the agent "submitted for approval", never "done"


async def approve(engine: AsyncEngine, action_id: str, approver: Approver) -> bool:
    """Returns True once the action has all the approvals it needs."""
    if "refund_approver" not in approver.roles:
        raise ApprovalError("missing role")
    async with engine.begin() as conn:  # COMMIT on success, ROLLBACK if anything below raises
        # Row lock so two approvers cannot race, and the conditions are checked atomically
        action = (await conn.execute(
            text("""
                SELECT * FROM pending_actions
                 WHERE id = :id AND status = 'pending' AND expires_at > now()
                 FOR UPDATE
            """),
            {"id": action_id},
        )).mappings().first()
        if action is None:
            raise ApprovalError("not pending or expired")
        if action["requested_by"] == approver.id:
            raise ApprovalError("requester cannot approve")
        if approver.id in (action["approved_by"] or []):
            raise ApprovalError("already approved by this person")

        approved_by = [*(action["approved_by"] or []), approver.id]
        done = len(approved_by) >= action["approvals_needed"]
        await conn.execute(
            text("UPDATE pending_actions SET approved_by = :by, status = :status WHERE id = :id"),
            {"id": action_id, "by": approved_by, "status": "approved" if done else "pending"},
        )
    return done

# A worker picks up 'approved' and 'auto_approved' rows and runs them through
# execute_once (24.14a), so a retried execution never repeats the side effect.
```

### Edge cases and failure modes

- **The reviewer sees less than the model did.** Show the reviewer the same retrieved documents and inputs the model used, plus what will change. A summary-only view produces rubber-stamping.
- **Data changed while pending.** A refund approved after the order was already refunded elsewhere is a double payment. Re-validate preconditions at execution time, not only at proposal time.
- **Agents that wait.** An agent loop cannot block for hours on an approval. End the run with a "pending approval" result and resume it from a webhook or job when the decision arrives.
- **Every approval is an audit event.** Write proposal, decision and execution to the audit trail in 26.4a, including who approved and what they saw.

## 26.7 Multi-Tenant Isolation

Enterprises often serve multiple internal organizations or external customers. Isolation strategies:

- **Logical isolation.** One database, tenant_id on every row, enforced by app.
- **Schema isolation.** One database, separate schemas per tenant.
- **Physical isolation.** Separate database per tenant.
- **Per-tenant deployments.** Entirely separate stacks.

For AI specifically:
- Per-tenant vector indices or filtered shared indices.
- Per-tenant prompts and configurations.
- Per-tenant rate limits and budgets.
- Per-tenant audit logs.

The right answer depends on the customer's contract and regulatory needs. SOC 2 logical isolation usually suffices; HIPAA and finance often demand physical.

## 26.7a SSO, Identity Propagation and Document Permissions ➕

Every isolation strategy above depends on knowing, on each request, which tenant and which user is asking; this section covers how enterprise identity (SSO) reaches the retrieval query and the agent's tools, which the chapter does not cover.

### Why AI features break identity assumptions

**Problem.** Enterprise customers log in through their own identity provider (Okta, Microsoft Entra ID and similar) with SAML or OpenID Connect. Classic apps check identity once per request. AI features break that in three ways:

1. **Retrieval crosses documents with different permissions.** A user may see the HR folder but not the board folder, both in the same tenant. A tenant filter alone leaks within the tenant.
2. **Agents act later and elsewhere.** A background agent run calls tools minutes after the request, often with a service account that can see everything (the "confused deputy" problem: the agent uses its broad rights on behalf of a user with narrow ones).
3. **Revocation lags.** Without SCIM provisioning, a deprovisioned employee keeps access until their session expires. With 8-hour sessions, that is up to 8 hours. If document permissions sync from the source system hourly, a revoked permission stays visible in retrieval for up to an hour.

### The mechanism

- Take tenant and user identity only from the verified token (OIDC ID token or your own session issued after SAML), never from request bodies or prompts.
- Enforce the tenant boundary in the database with Postgres row-level security, so a missing `WHERE` clause cannot leak.
- Store each chunk's allowed principals (users and groups copied from the source system's ACLs) and filter retrieval by the caller's principals.
- Give agent runs the user's identity and principals, and make every tool check them, instead of running tools as an all-powerful service account.
- Use SCIM to deprovision users and groups promptly, and shorten AI-session lifetimes for sensitive tenants.

### Implementation

```sql
ALTER TABLE chunks ENABLE ROW LEVEL SECURITY;
ALTER TABLE chunks FORCE ROW LEVEL SECURITY;  -- applies to the table owner too

CREATE POLICY tenant_isolation ON chunks
  USING (tenant_id = current_setting('app.tenant_id')::uuid);

-- allowed_principals: text[] of user and group IDs copied from the source ACL
CREATE INDEX ON chunks USING gin (allowed_principals);
```

```python
from collections.abc import AsyncIterator
from contextlib import asynccontextmanager
from dataclasses import dataclass

from sqlalchemy import text
from sqlalchemy.ext.asyncio import AsyncConnection, AsyncEngine


@dataclass(frozen=True, slots=True)
class Identity:  # from the verified token only
    tenant_id: str
    user_id: str
    groups: tuple[str, ...]


@asynccontextmanager
async def with_identity(engine: AsyncEngine, ident: Identity) -> AsyncIterator[AsyncConnection]:
    async with engine.begin() as conn:  # COMMIT on success, ROLLBACK on exception, then back to the pool
        # set_config(..., true) is transaction-local, so it is safe with pooled connections
        await conn.execute(text("SELECT set_config('app.tenant_id', :t, true)"), {"t": ident.tenant_id})
        yield conn


RETRIEVE = text("""
    SELECT id, text, 1 - (embedding <=> CAST(:qvec AS vector)) AS score
      FROM chunks
     WHERE allowed_principals && CAST(:principals AS text[])   -- document-level permission
     ORDER BY embedding <=> CAST(:qvec AS vector)
     LIMIT :k
""")  # no tenant filter needed here: RLS adds it


async def retrieve(engine: AsyncEngine, ident: Identity, query_embedding: list[float], k: int = 8):
    principals = [ident.user_id, *ident.groups]
    async with with_identity(engine, ident) as conn:
        result = await conn.execute(
            RETRIEVE, {"qvec": str(query_embedding), "principals": principals, "k": k},
        )
        return result.mappings().all()
```

### Edge cases and failure modes

- **Roles that bypass RLS.** Superusers and roles with `BYPASSRLS` ignore policies. Run the application as a plain role and test that a query without `app.tenant_id` set returns an error or no rows, never everything.
- **Group explosion.** Users in hundreds of groups make the principal list long and the filter slow. Resolve nested groups at sync time and cap what you store per chunk.
- **Caches and summaries.** A summary built from documents the next user cannot see leaks their content. Store the principals of the source chunks with any derived artefact, and filter on them too.
- **Background jobs have no session.** Ingestion and evaluation jobs need an explicit, narrowly scoped identity, logged in the audit trail, not a shared admin account.
- **Isolation tests.** Use the canary approach from [24.19a](/docs/genai-handbook/24-production-failures#2419a-canary-based-isolation-tests-) at both levels: across tenants and across permission groups inside one tenant.

## 26.8 Vendor Risk Management

AI introduces vendor concentration risk. If your product depends on one provider and they go down, raise prices, or change terms, you are exposed.

Mitigations:
- **Multi-provider** via LLM gateway.
- **Open model fallback** for critical paths.
- **Vendor SOC 2 / ISO 27001** review before signing.
- **Data processing agreements** specifying retention, training, and incident notification.
- **Exit strategy** documented — what would migration look like if needed.

## 26.8a Model Risk Management for LLM Systems ➕

Vendor risk covers the supplier; banks and insurers also have to manage the risk of the model's decisions themselves, under a discipline called model risk management that the chapter does not cover.

### Where the discipline comes from

**Problem.** In regulated finance, every "model" used in decisions must be inventoried, independently validated and monitored. The US supervisory guidance SR 11-7 (Federal Reserve and OCC, 2011) set the pattern that many banks worldwide follow. Risk teams now classify LLM features as models, and engineering teams find out late that a feature cannot launch without a validation report.

**Why LLMs are awkward here.** Classic validation checks a model's conceptual soundness and data. For an LLM you cannot inspect the weights or the training data of a hosted model, and the provider changes models on its own schedule. So the unit you validate is the **system**: model version, prompt, retrieval, tools and guardrails together, against a defined use.

### The mechanism

1. **Inventory with tiers.** Each LLM system has an entry: owner, use, model and prompt versions, risk tier, validation status and next review date.
2. **Independent validation.** A team that did not build the system tests it: performance on representative and adversarial cases, failure analysis, comparison with a challenger (a simpler model or the existing human process), and limits of use.
3. **Ongoing monitoring with thresholds.** Defined metrics (accuracy on sampled production cases, escalation rate, complaint rate) with thresholds that trigger review.
4. **Revalidation on material change.** A model upgrade or a major prompt rewrite is a material change.

### Implementation: block deploys of unvalidated systems

```python
import datetime as dt
from dataclasses import dataclass, field
from typing import Literal


@dataclass(frozen=True, slots=True)
class ValidatedConfig:
    model_id: str
    prompt_version: str


@dataclass(frozen=True, slots=True)
class MonitoringMetric:
    metric: str
    threshold: float
    current: float
    higher_is_better: bool


@dataclass(frozen=True, slots=True)
class ModelInventoryEntry:
    system_id: str
    tier: Literal[1, 2, 3]               # 1 = highest impact
    model_id: str
    prompt_version: str
    validated_config: ValidatedConfig | None
    validation_expires: dt.date | None
    monitoring: list[MonitoringMetric] = field(default_factory=list)


REVALIDATE_MONTHS: dict[int, int] = {1: 12, 2: 24, 3: 36}


@dataclass(frozen=True, slots=True)
class DeployDecision:
    allowed: bool
    reasons: list[str]
    revalidate_every_months: int


def deploy_allowed(e: ModelInventoryEntry, today: dt.date | None = None) -> DeployDecision:
    today = today or dt.date.today()
    reasons: list[str] = []
    if e.validated_config is None:
        reasons.append("never validated")
    elif e.validated_config != ValidatedConfig(e.model_id, e.prompt_version):
        reasons.append("config differs from the validated one (material change?)")
    if e.validation_expires and e.validation_expires < today:
        reasons.append("validation expired")
    for m in e.monitoring:
        breached = m.current < m.threshold if m.higher_is_better else m.current > m.threshold
        if breached:
            reasons.append(f"monitoring threshold breached: {m.metric}")
    return DeployDecision(not reasons, reasons, REVALIDATE_MONTHS[e.tier])
```

Run it in the deploy pipeline next to the eval gate. A blocked deploy with a clear reason is far cheaper than a finding from a regulator.

### Edge cases and failure modes

- **Provider-forced changes.** A provider retiring a model forces a material change on its schedule, not yours. Start revalidation as soon as a retirement date is announced.
- **Not every prompt edit is material.** Define in advance which changes need full revalidation and which need only the automated eval gate, or the process will block every typo fix.
- **Frameworks outside banking.** The NIST AI Risk Management Framework (Govern, Map, Measure, Manage) covers similar ground for non-financial organisations and maps well onto the same inventory.

## 26.9 Data Handling

Enterprise data handling for AI:
- **Classify** data sensitivity (public, internal, confidential, restricted).
- **Minimize** data sent to AI services — don't send what you don't need.
- **Mask or tokenize** PII before sending to external providers.
- **Document** data flows for each AI feature.
- **Honor** deletion requests across all systems including vector stores and backups.

## 26.9a Provider Data Retention, ZDR and Regional Processing ➕

The data-handling rules above govern your own systems; the model provider keeps its own copy of prompts and outputs under its own rules, and those rules need engineering controls too.

### What providers keep by default

**Problem.** A customer contract says "no customer data stored outside the EU" and "no retention by sub-processors". The team signs it, then sends prompts to a provider that keeps them for 30 days in another region.

**Why it happens.** As of October 2026, the defaults at the two largest API providers are:

- **Anthropic:** API inputs and outputs are deleted within 30 days, unless a different arrangement applies (such as a zero-data-retention agreement), or longer retention is needed to enforce the usage policy or comply with law. Zero data retention (ZDR) is granted by agreement, not by default. Some models are excluded: Anthropic's "covered models" (such as Claude Fable 5 and 5.1) require 30-day retention and are not offered under ZDR.
- **OpenAI:** abuse-monitoring logs are kept for up to 30 days by default. Approved customers can get Modified Abuse Monitoring or Zero Data Retention per organisation or project. OpenAI also offers EU data residency: projects created with the Europe region are processed in-region with zero data retention.

Two details catch teams out. First, **stateful features** (stored files, batch inputs and results, server-side conversation state) keep data by design, so ZDR may not cover them. Check each feature you use. Second, **regional processing has its own controls**: Anthropic exposes an `inference_geo` request parameter (for example `"us"`) at a price premium, and models served through AWS Bedrock, Google Vertex AI or Azure run in the cloud region you choose.

### The mechanism: a policy table that fails closed

Map each data class to the providers, models, regions and retention terms it may use. Every model call resolves its route through this table; if no route matches, the call fails instead of picking a default. The same table limits failover targets (see [24.16a](/docs/genai-handbook/24-production-failures#2416a-multi-provider-failover-in-code-)).

### Implementation

```python
from dataclasses import dataclass
from typing import Literal

type DataClass = Literal["public", "internal", "confidential", "restricted"]
type Region = Literal["us", "eu", "global"]


@dataclass(frozen=True, slots=True)
class Route:
    provider: Literal["anthropic", "openai", "bedrock"]
    model: str
    region: Region
    zdr: bool             # contract in place for this org or project
    stateless_only: bool  # no files, batches or stored conversation state


ROUTES: list[Route] = [
    Route("anthropic", "claude-opus-5-5", region="global", zdr=False, stateless_only=False),
    Route("bedrock", "anthropic.claude-opus-5-5", region="eu", zdr=True, stateless_only=True),
    Route("openai", "eu-project-default", region="eu", zdr=True, stateless_only=True),
]


@dataclass(frozen=True, slots=True)
class Requirement:
    regions: frozenset[Region]
    zdr: bool
    allow_stateful: bool


POLICY: dict[DataClass, Requirement] = {
    "public":       Requirement(frozenset({"us", "eu", "global"}), zdr=False, allow_stateful=True),
    "internal":     Requirement(frozenset({"us", "eu", "global"}), zdr=False, allow_stateful=True),
    "confidential": Requirement(frozenset({"eu"}), zdr=True, allow_stateful=False),
    "restricted":   Requirement(frozenset(), zdr=True, allow_stateful=False),  # no external provider at all
}


class NoApprovedRoute(Exception):
    pass


def allowed_routes(data_class: DataClass, needs_stateful_feature: bool) -> list[Route]:
    req = POLICY[data_class]
    routes = [
        r for r in ROUTES
        if r.region in req.regions
        and (not req.zdr or r.zdr)
        and (not needs_stateful_feature or (req.allow_stateful and not r.stateless_only))
    ]
    if not routes:
        raise NoApprovedRoute(f"no approved model route for {data_class} data")  # fail closed, never default
    return routes  # ordered: primary first, then failover candidates
```

The routes and model names here are placeholders; your legal team fills in the table from the actual contracts, and engineering owns the enforcement.

### Edge cases and failure modes

- **The data class must be known per request.** Tag it where data enters (per tenant, per document collection or per field) and carry it with the request. A request with no class should be treated as the strictest one.
- **Logs and traces leak around the policy.** Observability tools that capture full prompts are processors too. Apply the same table to them, or redact before export.
- **Contracts change.** Store the agreement reference and expiry next to each route, and alert before a ZDR agreement or DPA lapses.
- **New features quietly add retention.** Turning on a provider feature that stores data (file uploads, background responses) can break a ZDR promise. Review provider features with the same process as new vendors.

## 26.9b Private Networking to Model Providers ➕

Where the data goes is one question; how it travels there is the next one enterprise security reviews ask, and the chapter does not cover it.

### Why "over the public internet with TLS" is often not accepted

**Problem.** Security teams in banks, hospitals and government want production workloads to have no route to the public internet. A call to a public model API needs one.

**Why it matters.** TLS protects the content in transit, but an open egress path is also the path for data exfiltration from a compromised workload. Many enterprise network policies therefore block general internet egress and allow only private endpoints or a small allowlist through an inspected proxy.

### The mechanism: three options

1. **Cloud-hosted models through private endpoints.** Models on AWS Bedrock, Google Vertex AI or Azure can be reached through AWS PrivateLink interface endpoints, Google Private Service Connect or Azure Private Endpoints. Traffic stays on the cloud provider's network, and private DNS makes the normal SDK endpoint resolve to a private IP.
2. **First-party provider APIs through an egress proxy.** Route calls through a proxy or firewall that allows only the provider's API hostnames, logs every request, and blocks everything else. This is what most teams do for direct Anthropic or OpenAI API access.
3. **Self-hosted open models inside the VPC.** No external call at all, at the price of running inference yourself (see [25.12a](/docs/genai-handbook/25-cost-optimization#2512a-a-break-even-calculation-you-can-rerun-)).

### Implementation: a Bedrock interface endpoint (Terraform)

```hcl
resource "aws_security_group" "bedrock_endpoint" {
  name   = "bedrock-endpoint"
  vpc_id = var.vpc_id

  ingress {
    from_port       = 443
    to_port         = 443
    protocol        = "tcp"
    security_groups = [var.app_security_group_id] # only the app tier may call it
  }
}

resource "aws_vpc_endpoint" "bedrock_runtime" {
  vpc_id              = var.vpc_id
  service_name        = "com.amazonaws.${var.region}.bedrock-runtime"
  vpc_endpoint_type   = "Interface"
  subnet_ids          = var.private_subnet_ids
  security_group_ids  = [aws_security_group.bedrock_endpoint.id]
  private_dns_enabled = true # the SDK's default hostname now resolves to private IPs
}
```

With private DNS on, application code does not change: the SDK calls the usual regional hostname and the VPC resolves it to the endpoint. Check that the specific Bedrock API your SDK uses is served by the endpoint service you created, since Bedrock exposes more than one runtime endpoint.

### Edge cases and failure modes

- **Failover breaks the network policy.** A failover route to a first-party API (24.16a) needs internet egress that the private design removed. Either allow it explicitly through the proxy or remove that route for tenants that require private networking.
- **Streaming through proxies.** Inspecting proxies may buffer server-sent events or cut long connections. Test streaming and long reasoning responses through the real proxy path.
- **Endpoints cost money per zone.** Interface endpoints are billed per hour per availability zone plus per GB processed. Small, but multiply by regions, zones and environments.
- **Endpoint policies.** Attach an endpoint policy or IAM conditions so only approved models and accounts can be invoked through the endpoint, otherwise any workload in the VPC can call any model.

## 26.11a A Vendor Evaluation Scorecard for Model Providers 🔧

Procurement asks engineering for a recommendation; a scorecard with knock-out criteria and weights makes that recommendation repeatable and defensible.

### Why a plain feature comparison fails

**Problem.** Model vendors are compared on benchmark scores and price per token. Six months later the chosen vendor cannot sign the data terms a big customer needs, or retires the model with 60 days' notice.

**Why it happens.** For enterprise use, several criteria are pass or fail: if the vendor cannot meet them, no score elsewhere compensates. Averaging them into a weighted total hides that. A vendor scoring 9 out of 10 on quality and 0 on data residency still averages well.

### The mechanism

1. **Knock-out criteria first.** Any "no" removes the vendor: signed DPA with no training on your data, retention terms you can accept (ZDR where needed), processing regions you need, a current SOC 2 Type II report, a BAA if you handle US health data.
2. **Weighted criteria second.** Quality on your own eval set (never public benchmarks alone), cost per resolved task, latency at your p95, rate-limit headroom, model deprecation notice period, uptime history, private networking options, IP indemnity, ISO/IEC 27001 and 42001 certification, admin features (SSO for the console, usage APIs, audit logs).
3. **Evidence per score.** Each score links to the document or test run behind it, so the evaluation survives an audit and can be repeated next year.

### Implementation

```python
from dataclasses import dataclass, field
from typing import Literal

type Knockout = Literal["dpa_no_training", "acceptable_retention", "required_regions", "soc2_type_ii"]


@dataclass(frozen=True, slots=True)
class Vendor:
    name: str
    knockouts: dict[Knockout, bool]
    scores: dict[str, float]                               # 0..10, each backed by evidence
    evidence: dict[str, str] = field(default_factory=dict)  # criterion -> link to report, test run or contract clause


WEIGHTS: dict[str, float] = {
    "eval_quality": 0.30,        # your own eval set
    "cost_per_task": 0.20,
    "latency_p95": 0.10,
    "rate_limit_headroom": 0.10,
    "deprecation_notice": 0.10,
    "private_networking": 0.05,
    "certifications": 0.05,      # ISO/IEC 27001, 42001
    "indemnity": 0.05,
    "admin_features": 0.05,
}


@dataclass(frozen=True, slots=True)
class Ranking:
    name: str
    eligible: bool
    failed: list[str]
    missing_evidence: list[str]
    score: float


def rank_vendors(vendors: list[Vendor]) -> list[Ranking]:
    ranked = []
    for v in vendors:
        failed = [k for k, ok in v.knockouts.items() if not ok]
        missing_evidence = [k for k in WEIGHTS if not v.evidence.get(k)]
        score = sum(w * v.scores.get(k, 0) for k, w in WEIGHTS.items())
        ranked.append(Ranking(v.name, not failed, failed, missing_evidence, round(score, 2)))
    return sorted(ranked, key=lambda r: (not r.eligible, -r.score))
```

### Edge cases and failure modes

- **Scores go stale.** Model quality and prices change every few months. Rerun the eval-based scores quarterly; contract terms yearly or on renewal.
- **One vendor for everything.** Different use cases have different knock-outs. Score per use case (or per data class from 26.9a), which often leads naturally to two approved vendors, which also covers the concentration risk in 26.8.
- **Weights decided after the scores.** Agree weights before testing, or the scorecard gets tuned to justify a favourite.
- **The cloud marketplace route.** Buying a model through your existing cloud contract (Bedrock, Vertex AI, Azure) can reuse an existing DPA and spend commitment, which can remove months of procurement. Score that path separately from the direct API.
