Across this series a lot of rules were simply stated: config goes in environment variables, the server binds a port, logs go to stdout, processes are stateless, killing a process must lose nothing. **This lesson justifies them.** Nearly all of them come from one document, the **Twelve-Factor App**.

## 1. What the Twelve-Factor App is 🎓

### A contract between your app and the platform
In one sentence: **it is a contract between your application and the platform that runs it.** It lists what must be true about your app so that **any machine, any platform and any new teammate** can run it, scale it and update it with no surprises.

- It was written around **2011** by the people running **Heroku**, one of the first cloud platforms.
- Every platform you deploy to today **assumes you follow it**, directly or indirectly: Heroku first (they wrote it), then Docker, Kubernetes, Render, Railway, Fly, Cloud Run, App Runner, Vercel, Netlify.
- Many of the rules are now so normal that people follow them without knowing their origin. When a platform says "we inject config through the environment" or "write logs to stdout and we'll collect them", that is this document speaking.
- **Why follow it:** if your app already meets the platform's expectations, you stop fighting the platform. Moving between platforms becomes easy.

### Scope 🎓
- **In scope:** apps that run **as a service**: web apps, APIs, anything reachable over a network. Any language, any database, any queue.
- **Out of scope:** desktop software and mobile apps.
- The factors are about the **interface between the app and the platform**. They are **not** a list of everything that's good in software: DRY or KISS aren't factors. And this lesson stays away from Kubernetes operations and microservice architecture.

### Who wrote it, and why it was needed 🎓
- **Platform as a service (PaaS)** means you hand the platform your code, and it does everything else: builds it, prepares the environment and config, connects the database, sets up networking, until the app is live on a domain.
- Being in that business, Heroku saw **thousands of apps** in many languages and frameworks get deployed, scaled, break and get corrupted. That visibility is what let **Adam Wiggins**, a Heroku co-founder, write the patterns down as twelve factors and publish them at **12factor.net in 2011**.

### The world before: snowflake servers and erosion 🎓
Deploying in that era looked like this:

1. Rent a server, or keep a **physical machine in the office** (cloud existed but wasn't the default).
2. **Install the OS by hand** (say Ubuntu), then the **language runtime** by hand (Java, Go, Python...).
3. Install the **database** and supporting libraries.
4. **Copy the app over**, often with **FTP**.
5. **Edit config files on the server** over **SSH**.

Every server built this way was a **snowflake**: unique, hand-built and **impossible to reproduce**. Another engineer following the same written steps would almost certainly end up with something slightly different. If the machine died, you rebuilt it from memory or a manual. A new hire needed **days** to run the app locally, and their copy still didn't match production. Hence **"it works on my machine"**, and teams **freezing all other work on deploy day**.

The document's name for what happens to such an app is **software erosion**: the app slowly **stops working even though nobody changed its code**. For example:
- the OS gets patched by a distribution update;
- **another app on the same server** upgrades a shared library and breaks yours;
- the person who set it all up **leaves**, and their undocumented manual changes leave with them.

**The twelve factors are your defence against erosion.**

<Mermaid chart={`flowchart TD
  S["Hand-built snowflake server"] --> E1["OS patched under you"]
  S --> E2["Neighbour app upgrades a shared library"]
  S --> E3["Only one person knows the setup"]
  E1 & E2 & E3 --> X["Erosion: app breaks with no code change"]
  X --> F["Twelve factors: make every input<br/>declared, versioned and reproducible"]`} />

### The document today: frozen, then open-sourced 🎓
- For about **13 years** it barely changed (the footer said **last updated 2017**, and that update was small). Meanwhile containers, Kubernetes, serverless and managed cloud services took over.
- In **November 2024**, Heroku (owned by **Salesforce** today) **open-sourced** it: moved it into a public repo under a neutral organization, wrote a governance document and invited maintainers from other companies, including **AWS and Google Cloud**. It was announced on stage at **KubeCon, November 2024**.
- The community rewrite is happening on a branch called **`next`**. It keeps all twelve names and one-line mottos, but rewrites each factor as a **numbered list of principles**, separating timeless ideas from 2011-era details.
- At recording time the live site still showed the **classic 2011 text**; the rewrite was an unmerged draft.

**The lesson's plan for each factor:** what it says in plain words, the problem it fixed, where the series already used it, what the rewrite changes, and whether it aged well.

## 2. Factor I: one codebase, many deploys 🎓

### What it says
One codebase, tracked in version control, deployed many times.

### Part 1: "tracked in revision control"
Obvious today (Git with GitHub, GitLab or Bitbucket). It had to be written down in 2011 because many production apps were **a folder on someone's computer** or a zip called something like `final_v2_really_final.zip`, emailed between teammates.

### Part 2: "many deploys" (the less obvious idea)
A **deploy** is one **running instance** of the app: production is a deploy, staging is a deploy, a per-PR preview is a deploy, your laptop is a deploy.

**Problem:** production must talk to a real database and your laptop to a local Docker Postgres. Something has to differ between deploys. **What?**
- **Not the code.** Deploys may run **different commits**, but every commit lives in **the same repository**.
- **The config** is what differs. **Code is shared by every deploy; config is unique to each deploy.**

The lesson's example:

| Deploy | Running | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Repository | commit 100 | latest on main |
| Production | commit 97 | deployed last Friday |
| Staging | commit 99 | being tested for tomorrow's release |
| Your laptop | commit 100 + 2 unpushed | work in progress |

On release day, staging moves to 100, then production moves to 100. **Nothing about the deploys changed except which commit of the same repo each one runs.**

**Test:** if you can't describe every environment as "this codebase at commit X (plus its config)", something is drifting.

<Mermaid chart={`flowchart LR
  REPO[("One repo")] --> C97["commit 97"] & C99["commit 99"] & C100["commit 100+"]
  C97 --> P["Production<br/>+ prod config"]
  C99 --> S["Staging<br/>+ staging config"]
  C100 --> L["Laptop<br/>+ local config"]`} />

### The two boundaries
1. **Several codebases = a distributed system, not one app.** Each component is its own app and follows the twelve factors on its own.
2. **Two apps sharing code by copy-paste is a violation.** Shared code should become a **library** consumed through the dependency manager (that's Factor II).

### 2026: monorepos 🎓
People often read this factor as "monorepos are an anti-pattern". The lesson disagrees. The definition counts a codebase as a repo, or a set of repos sharing a root commit, and the test is **one codebase ↔ one app**. A monorepo that builds five apps is simply five codebases that happen to share a repository, each with its own twelve factors. **What the factor forbids is the same app living in two places** (copy-pasted code), not many apps living in one place.

### 🔧 Going deeper: making "which commit is this deploy?" answerable
Stamp the commit into the build and expose it, so every running deploy can tell you what it is:

```ts title="src/version.controller.ts"
@Controller()
export class VersionController {
  @Get('version')
  version() {
    return { commit: process.env.GIT_SHA ?? 'dev', builtAt: process.env.BUILT_AT ?? null };
  }
}
```

```dockerfile
ARG GIT_SHA=dev
# baked at build time: identifies the build, not the deploy
ENV GIT_SHA=$GIT_SHA
```

## 3. Factor II: explicitly declare and isolate dependencies 🎓

Two key words: **declare** and **isolate**.

### Declare
Somewhere in the repo, one file lists **completely and accurately** every library the app needs: `package.json` (Node), `go.mod` (Go), `requirements.txt` or `pyproject.toml` (Python), `Gemfile` (Ruby). This is the **manifest**.

- **Problem without it:** a new laptop or new teammate has to read every import, guess versions, and untangle version clashes. That can take **2 to 3 days**.
- **With it:** setup is **one command** that downloads and installs everything.
- 2011 named `Gemfile` + `bundle install` (Rails era). Today the idea is **stronger** thanks to **lock files** (`package-lock.json`, `go.sum`...).

**Why a manifest alone isn't enough:** a manifest states **ranges** ("roughly this version"). A **lock file** pins the **exact** version of every library **and of every library's dependencies** (the transitive tree). Months or years later, an install from the lock file pulls exactly the same versions.

```json title="package.json (manifest: ranges)"
{ "dependencies": { "@nestjs/core": "^11.0.0", "pg": "^8.13.0" } }
```

```bash
npm ci    # 🔧 installs EXACTLY what package-lock.json says, fails if it disagrees with package.json
```

### Isolate
**Problem:** your `requirements.txt` asks for `requests` v2, but the app runs against the **system Python**, where another tool already installed `requests` v1. The import finds the system copy first. **You declared correctly and still ran the wrong code.**

- **Declare without isolate:** declarations are ignored; whatever is on the machine leaks in.
- **Isolate without declare:** you spend days trying to reproduce versions you never wrote down.
- **Declaration feels natural** (you can't work without it). **Isolation is the one people forget**: nothing from the host machine should leak into the app.
- 2011 tools: **`bundle exec`** (Ruby) and **`virtualenv`** (Python), which make the app see only declared dependencies.

```bash
# Python, labelled: isolation with a virtual environment
python -m venv .venv && . .venv/bin/activate
pip install -r requirements.txt     # installs into .venv, not the system Python
```

### System tools leak too: the ImageMagick example 🎓
An image-resizing backend shells out to ImageMagick.
- Your Mac (Homebrew) has **ImageMagick 7**, whose main binary is **`magick`**.
- The Ubuntu server has **ImageMagick 6**, whose binary is **`convert`**, with different defaults.
- **Same code, different programs, different behaviour.** Two major versions with breaking changes.

The 2011 text's answer: **vendor** such tools into the app instead of relying on the host.

### The modern answer: containers 🎓
A Docker image freezes not only the libraries but also the **OS packages, system tools and the runtime itself**. That's the strongest declaration and isolation possible, which is why people say **Docker practically solved this factor**.

```dockerfile title="Dockerfile (🔧 declare + isolate everything)"
# pinned runtime and OS (pin by digest for full reproducibility)
FROM node:22.11-bookworm-slim
# the system tool is declared in the image, not assumed on the host
RUN apt-get update \
 && apt-get install -y --no-install-recommends imagemagick \
 && rm -rf /var/lib/apt/lists/*
WORKDIR /app
COPY package.json package-lock.json ./
# lock-file install
RUN npm ci --omit=dev
COPY dist ./dist
CMD ["node", "dist/main.js"]
```

🔧 **Edge cases:** Dockerfiles only allow comments on their own line (a trailing `#` after an instruction becomes part of its arguments). `apt-get install imagemagick` still resolves to whatever the mirror has today; pinning the base image by **digest** (`node:22.11-bookworm-slim@sha256:...`) and rebuilding deliberately is what freezes it. Pinning an exact apt version is possible but breaks when the distribution drops that version from its mirror. A `postinstall` script that downloads binaries at install time is an undeclared dependency hiding inside a declared one.

## 4. Factor III: store config in the environment 🎓

The most quoted and most argued factor.

### What counts as config
**Anything likely to differ between deploys:**

| Thing | Laptop | Production |
|---|---|---|
| Database URL | Postgres in Docker on localhost | Amazon RDS over the VPC |
| Email | sandbox API, test inbox | real provider |
| Object storage | local MinIO | AWS S3 |
| Redis | a container | a managed instance |

Only the config changes; the code is identical.

### The test: could you open-source it right now?
**Could you make your production codebase public this minute without leaking a single credential?** Not *would* you, but *could* you. If the answer is no, config is living inside your code.

### Why environment variables
1. **Easy to change per deploy** without touching a file.
2. **Hard to commit by accident.** Credentials committed to repos are how secrets leak every day.
3. **An OS-level standard**, independent of language or framework: the same mechanism works for Go, Node, Rust, Python or a bash script.

Alternatives of the time were per-framework files (Java `.properties`, Rails `config/database.yml`), and nearly all of them ended up committed, with credentials inside.

### The series' own example: Tasker (Go) 🎓
The Tasker task-management app declares its whole config as **one Go struct**: server, database, auth, Redis, email, AWS, observability, cron... about **38 values**, all loaded from env vars with a **`TASKER_` prefix** (`TASKER_SERVER_PORT`, `TASKER_DATABASE_HOST`...). Git history shows the **values changed many times** as integrations, timeouts and hosts were added, while **the code that reads them barely changed**. That's the point: **things that change at different rates shouldn't live in the same place.**

### Don't group config into named environments 🎓
2011 warned against files like `config/environments/staging.rb`, `production.rb`...
- **Problem:** it doesn't scale. Someone needs "staging, but pointing at a different database" for a PR branch, and now you add **code** for a new environment, every time.
- **Mechanism:** each value is an **independent, granular env var**, never tied to a mode name. A new deploy is **composed by setting values**, not by picking a mode. The rewrite keeps this rule.

### 🔧 Implementation: one typed, validated config module (NestJS)

```ts title="src/config/env.ts"
import { z } from 'zod';

export const Env = z.object({
  NODE_ENV: z.enum(['development', 'test', 'production']).default('development'), // runtime tuning only, never "which DB"
  PORT: z.coerce.number().int().default(3000),
  DATABASE_URL: z.string().url(),
  REDIS_URL: z.string().url(),
  S3_BUCKET: z.string().min(1),
  S3_ENDPOINT: z.string().url().optional(),        // set to MinIO locally, unset on AWS
  EMAIL_API_BASE: z.string().url(),
  REQUEST_TIMEOUT_MS: z.coerce.number().int().default(10_000),
});
export type Env = z.infer<typeof Env>;
```

```ts title="src/app.module.ts"
@Module({
  imports: [
    ConfigModule.forRoot({
      isGlobal: true,
      ignoreEnvFile: process.env.NODE_ENV === 'production', // .env is a laptop convenience only
      validate: (raw) => Env.parse(raw),                   // crash at boot, not at the first request
    }),
  ],
})
export class AppModule {}
```

- **Fail fast:** a missing `DATABASE_URL` should stop the process at startup with a clear message, not cause a 500 an hour later.
- Commit a **`.env.example`** with names and dummy values; **git-ignore `.env`**.
- Read config in **one module**. Scattered `process.env.X` calls are hard to audit.

### The controversy: env vars and secrets 🎓
Fine for a database **hostname**. But the **password**? In **2017, Diogo Mónica** (then leading security at Docker) argued **not to put secrets in env vars**, because the environment **eventually leaks**:
- **Child processes inherit the whole environment.** Shell out to anything and every secret goes with it.
- **Crash reporters and debug pages** often dump the full environment.
- On Linux, a process's environment is readable at **`/proc/PID/environ`** by anyone with sufficient access, and various tools log it.

**The alternative:** a **secret manager**. The app fetches credentials **at boot**, keeps them **in memory**, and they get **rotated and audited** there.

**Where the lesson lands:**
- **Separating config from code is non-negotiable**, then and now.
- **Env vars are the right default for everything that isn't a secret.**
- Solo or small team: secrets injected as env vars by your **platform's secrets feature** are acceptable, and far better than committing them.
- As the team grows: a **secret manager** (Vault, or AWS **Parameter Store**) is the no-brainer. Fetch at startup, keep in memory, rotate periodically, audit.

<Mermaid chart={`sequenceDiagram
  autonumber
  participant P as Platform
  participant A as App process
  participant SM as Secret manager
  P->>A: start with non-secret env (SECRET_ID, region)
  A->>SM: GetSecretValue(SECRET_ID) using the task role
  SM-->>A: db password (audited read)
  Note over A: kept in memory only, never in env or files
  A->>A: connect pool, begin serving`} />

```ts title="src/config/secrets.ts (🔧 fetch at boot)"
import { SecretsManagerClient, GetSecretValueCommand } from '@aws-sdk/client-secrets-manager';

export async function loadDbPassword(): Promise<string> {
  const sm = new SecretsManagerClient({});                       // credentials come from the IAM role, not env
  const out = await sm.send(new GetSecretValueCommand({ SecretId: process.env.DB_SECRET_ID! }));
  return JSON.parse(out.SecretString!).password;
}
```

🔧 **Edge cases:** after **rotation**, existing pool connections keep working but new ones fail with the old password. Re-fetch the secret on an auth error, or rotate with two valid credentials overlapping. In Node, `child_process.spawn` inherits `process.env` by default; pass an explicit `env` to child processes.

## 5. Factor IV: treat backing services as attached resources 🎓

### What a backing service is
**Anything the app consumes over the network:** Postgres, Redis, a queue, an SMTP or email service, S3, a payment provider. If the app talks to it over a protocol, it's a backing service.

### The rule
**The code makes no distinction between a service you run and one a third party runs.** Each is an **attached resource** reached through a **resource handle** = **URL + credentials**, and (Factor III) the handle lives in **config**.

**Test from the 2011 text:** you can swap your local Postgres for a managed one (Neon, PlanetScale, Supabase, RDS) **without changing a line of code**, only the handle.

### Anatomy of a handle 🎓

```text
postgres://app_user:s3cret@db.internal.example.com:5432/orders
└─scheme─┘ └─user─┘ └pass┘ └──────────host─────────┘ └port┘ └─db──┘
```

1. **scheme**: speaks the Postgres wire protocol
2. **user and password**
3. **host**
4. **port** (Postgres default **5432**)
5. **database name**

That one string is everything the app needs to attach the resource.

<Mermaid chart={`flowchart LR
  APP["Same code"] -->|"DATABASE_URL"| H{"Which handle?"}
  H -->|laptop| L["postgres in Docker"]
  H -->|staging| N["Neon"]
  H -->|production| R["Amazon RDS"]`} />

### It aged very well 🎓
In 2011 it sounded odd: "the database is somebody else's computer, reached by URL". Most people ran the server and the database on **the same machine**. A decade and a half later it's the default, and **co-locating app and database is considered an anti-pattern for scaling**. Managed Postgres, managed Redis, S3 for files, Resend for email instead of your own SMTP. The factor was almost a prediction.

### 🔧 Implementation and edge cases

```ts title="src/db/data-source.ts"
export const dataSource = new DataSource({
  type: 'postgres',
  url: config.DATABASE_URL,                         // the whole handle, nothing hard-coded
  ssl: config.DATABASE_URL.includes('sslmode=require') ? { rejectUnauthorized: true } : false,
  extra: { max: 10, connectionTimeoutMillis: 5_000 },
});
```

- **Detach and reattach:** because only the handle changes, failing over to a replica or restoring a backup to a new host is a config change plus restart.
- **Hidden coupling breaks the test:** code that checks `if (host === 'localhost')`, or S3 code that assumes path-style URLs, means the services aren't really interchangeable. Pass the endpoint as config (`S3_ENDPOINT` for MinIO).
- Passwords containing `@`, `:` or `/` must be **URL-encoded** inside the handle.

## 6. Factor V: strictly separate build, release and run 🎓

### The three stages (mapped to a CI/CD pipeline)
1. **Build:** the pipeline checks out the code **at a specific commit** (deploys are driven by commits now, not by FTP or SSH copies), fetches dependencies from the manifest (Factor II), compiles if needed, and produces one executable bundle: **the build**. For Go, a single binary; for Node, the app plus `node_modules`; in the container world, **an image**. **A build contains code and dependencies, never config.**
2. **Release:** take a build and combine it with **one deploy's config** (production, staging, a PR preview). Build + production config = the production release. **Same build** + staging config = the staging release.
3. **Run:** launch processes from a release.

<Mermaid chart={`flowchart LR
  C["commit 97"] --> B["BUILD<br/>image sha256:ab12"]
  B --> R1["RELEASE v100<br/>build ab12 + prod config A"]
  B --> R2["RELEASE v101<br/>build ab12 + prod config B<br/>(password rotated)"]
  C2["new commit"] --> B2["BUILD<br/>image sha256:cd34"]
  B2 --> R3["RELEASE v102<br/>build cd34 + prod config B"]
  R3 -. "errors spike: re-run v101" .-> R2
  R1 & R2 & R3 --> RUN["RUN<br/>start processes"]`} />

### Why be strict? Benefit 1: instant, free rollbacks 🎓
Builds are **immutable** and a release is just build + config, so going back costs nothing.
- Releases are an **append-only ledger**: each has an **ID** (a timestamp or an incrementing number) and is **never modified** once created.

The lesson's scenario:
- **Afternoon:** build commit 97 with the current config → **release 100**.
- **Two hours later:** someone rotates the database password. Config changed → **release 101** (same build, new config).
- **Two hours after that:** new code is merged → **release 102** (new build, same config).
- Release 102 produces a wave of errors on the dashboard and angry users in Slack. **Don't debug in production while customers wait. Re-run release 101.** It goes live in seconds because nothing has to be rebuilt.
- Once production is stable, debug release 102 at your own pace.

Release 101 still means exactly what it meant two hours ago, because it's the same build and the same config. Heroku releases, Docker image tags and Kubernetes rollouts all use this pattern.

### Benefit 2: no path from production back into the code 🎓
Code enters **only at build**. There is **no way to edit code on a running server** that feeds back into a build. The snowflake habit (SSH in, hot-patch a file, reload) is exactly what made production and the repository drift into **two different states**. Strict separation makes that impossible.

### What the rewrite adds 🎓
**Immutability**, implied in 2011, becomes an explicit, mandatory principle: **each release is a unique, immutable snapshot.**

### 🔧 Implementation on AWS: the image digest is the build, the task definition revision is the release

```yaml title=".github/workflows/release.yml (sketch)"
- name: Build once
  run: |
    docker build --build-arg GIT_SHA=${{ github.sha }} -t $ECR_REPO:${{ github.sha }} .
    docker push $ECR_REPO:${{ github.sha }}
- name: Release = build + this deploy's config
  run: |
    DIGEST=$(aws ecr describe-images --repository-name app --image-ids imageTag=${{ github.sha }} \
             --query 'imageDetails[0].imageDigest' --output text)
    jq --arg img "$ECR_REPO@$DIGEST" '.containerDefinitions[0].image = $img' taskdef.prod.json > td.json
    aws ecs register-task-definition --cli-input-json file://td.json   # new, immutable revision
```

- Each `register-task-definition` creates a numbered, immutable **revision**: that's your release ledger. **Rollback** = point the service at the previous revision.
- **Edge cases:** a rollback of **code** can't undo a **database migration** that already ran. Make migrations backward-compatible (expand, then contract) so release N−1 still works against the new schema. And deploy by **digest**, not a mutable tag like `latest`, or "re-running release 101" might pull different bytes.

## 7. Factor VI: execute the app as one or more stateless processes 🎓

### What it says
The app runs as processes that **remember nothing between requests**. This statelessness is what the series has relied on for scaling all along.

- **Share nothing** between processes: no shared memory, no shared disk.
- Anything that must persist goes to a **stateful backing service** (Factor IV).
- Assume **any request can be served by any process**, and **any process can disappear at any moment**.

### What a process *may* hold
**Memory and local disk as a cache for a single transaction.** Example: download a file, process it, write the result to the database. The temp file used during processing is fine, because **nothing after that transaction expects it to exist**, and temp storage can vanish anyway. It becomes a problem only when **a future request or process depends on that file being there**.

### Sticky sessions: the classic violation 🎓
1. The app stores the logged-in user's **session in process memory**. With one instance, it works.
2. You scale to **three instances behind a load balancer**. A user logs in on instance 1; the next request lands on instance 2, which has never heard of them. **Random logouts.**
3. The workaround: **sticky sessions**, where the load balancer remembers which instance each user belongs to and keeps routing them there.

The document says sticky sessions are a **violation, never to be used**. Why: stickiness **defeats the point of having three instances**.
- Kill (or lose) an instance and **all its users are logged out**.
- Add an instance for load and it **gets no existing users**.

**Fix:** a session is state, and state belongs in a backing service: **Redis or Postgres**, depending on your latency needs.

<Mermaid chart={`flowchart TB
  subgraph Bad["Session in process memory"]
    LB1["LB (sticky)"] --> I1["instance 1<br/>holds session"]
    LB1 -.-> I2["instance 2<br/>never heard of user"]
  end
  subgraph Good["Session in a backing service"]
    LB2["LB (round robin)"] --> J1["instance 1"] & J2["instance 2"] & J3["instance 3"]
    J1 & J2 & J3 --> RS[("Redis sessions")]
  end`} />

```ts title="src/main.ts (🔧 sessions in Redis, so any instance can serve any user)"
import session from 'express-session';
import { RedisStore } from 'connect-redis';
import { createClient } from 'redis';

const redis = createClient({ url: config.REDIS_URL });
await redis.connect();

app.use(session({
  store: new RedisStore({ client: redis, prefix: 'sess:' }),
  secret: sessionSecret,                    // from the secret manager
  resave: false,
  saveUninitialized: false,
  cookie: { httpOnly: true, secure: true, sameSite: 'lax', maxAge: 7 * 24 * 3600 * 1000 },
}));
```

Stateless JWTs are another option: the state travels in the token, so no server-side store is needed (at the cost of harder revocation).

### The honest exception: WebSockets 🎓
In the real-time lesson, a WebSocket connection **lives in one process's memory**, so only that instance can talk to that user. The fix there was **pub/sub**: every event is published, and whichever instance holds the user's socket subscribes and forwards it. The lesson's position: **statelessness is the default everywhere, and you may break it when the feature itself is the state** (real-time connections).

```ts title="🔧 Socket.IO across instances via Redis pub/sub"
import { createAdapter } from '@socket.io/redis-adapter';
const pub = createClient({ url: config.REDIS_URL }); const sub = pub.duplicate();
await Promise.all([pub.connect(), sub.connect()]);
io.adapter(createAdapter(pub, sub));       // io.to(room).emit(...) now reaches sockets on every instance
```

### The thumb rule 🎓
**If you can't kill any process at any moment without a user losing data, you have state where it doesn't belong.**

🔧 **Other hidden state to look for:** in-memory rate-limit counters (each instance counts separately, so the real limit is N times higher), in-process cron jobs (run N times, once per instance), uploaded files written to local disk, in-memory caches that must agree across instances.

## 8. Mid-point recap 🎓

The contract so far:
- Code lives in **one repository**.
- Dependencies are **declared and isolated**.
- **Everything that differs between deploys lives in the environment.**
- Neighbours (backing services) are **attached through handles**.
- The way to production is **build → release → run**.
- The app runs as **disposable, forgetful processes**.

Six factors in, the service is already portable to any platform. Now the other six.

## 9. Factor VII: export services via port binding 🎓

### The 2011 problem
**PHP** (the "React of its day") didn't run by itself: **Apache** ran, and the PHP app was a **module loaded inside Apache**. A **Java** app was a file dropped into **Tomcat's** folder. The web server was the program; your app was a guest. So the **web server's version and config became part of your app's runtime**, living on the physical server. **Erosion again.**

### What it says
The app is **completely self-contained**: it brings its **own HTTP server as a library** (declared in the Factor II manifest) and **exports itself by binding to a port** and listening.

- Local: hit `localhost:8080` (Go) or `localhost:4000` (Node) directly.
- Production: a **routing layer** (reverse proxy or load balancer) maps the public hostname to the process's port.
- **Consequence:** anything that binds a port can be **a backing service for another app**. Database, cache and email service all bind ports locally; in production only the handles change to someone else's port on someone else's machine.

```ts title="src/main.ts"
async function bootstrap() {
  const app = await NestFactory.create(AppModule);   // the HTTP server is a library dependency
  app.enableShutdownHooks();                         // see Factor IX
  await app.listen(Number(process.env.PORT ?? 3000), '0.0.0.0'); // 🔧 0.0.0.0, or it's unreachable inside a container
}
bootstrap();
```

<Mermaid chart={`flowchart LR
  U["api.example.com:443"] --> RP["Reverse proxy / ALB<br/>TLS termination"]
  RP -->|"PORT=3000"| A1["app process"]
  RP -->|"PORT=3000"| A2["app process"]
  A1 & A2 -->|"handle"| DB["postgres:5432"]
  A1 & A2 -->|"handle"| RD["redis:6379"]`} />

### What aged: serverless 🎓
An **AWS Lambda** function binds no port. The platform **calls a function you export**. In 2011, long-running port-bound servers looked like the permanent shape of things; serverless added a whole other category. So the rewrite keeps port binding in the headline but generalises: **the app is self-contained and exports its service through a declared interface**. That covers both a port-bound server and an exported function.

```ts title="🔧 the same idea, serverless: the interface is an exported handler"
export const handler = async (event: APIGatewayProxyEventV2) => ({
  statusCode: 200,
  body: JSON.stringify({ ok: true }),
});
```

## 10. The rest of the contract on serverless ➕

Factor VII only rewrote one factor for serverless; the other factors still apply to a function, but several of them change shape, and server habits carried into a function break in quiet ways.

**The model to reason from (AWS Lambda, the default mode):** your code runs inside an **execution environment**, a small isolated sandbox. It goes through three phases: **Init** (start the runtime and run your module-level code, limited to **10 seconds** for on-demand functions), **Invoke** (run the handler, once per event), and **Shutdown**. Between invocations the environment is **frozen, not destroyed**, and the next event may thaw and reuse it. **One environment handles one event at a time**; to serve 50 concurrent requests, Lambda runs 50 environments. Lambda also recycles environments every few hours, even busy ones.

<Mermaid chart={`sequenceDiagram
  autonumber
  participant L as Lambda service
  participant E as Execution environment
  L->>E: Init phase, runtime start + module-level code (max 10 s)
  L->>E: Invoke event 1 (handler runs)
  Note over E: frozen between events, memory and /tmp kept
  L->>E: Invoke event 2 (warm, module scope still there)
  L->>E: Shutdown (no handler call, then killed)
  Note over L,E: a second concurrent request gets a different environment`} />

Cold starts and the database-connection explosion are covered in [Scaling and Performance, part 2](/docs/backend-first-principles/22-scaling-performance-part-2). Here the question is narrower: **which factor does each serverless rule come from?**

#### Factor VI on serverless: state that survives by accident ➕
- **Problem:** a function "works" in testing, then in production one user sometimes sees another user's data, or a counter drifts.
- **Why it happens:** variables declared **outside the handler** live as long as the environment, so they survive across invocations, but only inside **that one** environment. That is the worst mix: state that persists often enough to be relied on, but not reliably enough to be correct. It is the in-process session from Factor VI with a different cause.
- **Mechanism:** use module scope only for things that are **the same for every request**: SDK clients, a connection pool, parsed config, a cache of public data. Everything per request or per user lives inside the handler, and anything that must survive goes to a backing service (Factor IV).

```ts title="handler.ts (module scope: shared objects only)"
import { S3Client, GetObjectCommand } from '@aws-sdk/client-s3';

const s3 = new S3Client({});                  // ✅ same for every request: create once per environment
let requestCount = 0;                          // ❌ looks global, is per environment: useless as a metric
let currentUser: string | undefined;           // ❌ leaks between users if any path forgets to reset it

export const handler = async (event: { userId: string; key: string }) => {
  const user = event.userId;                   // ✅ per-request state stays in handler scope
  const obj = await s3.send(new GetObjectCommand({ Bucket: process.env.BUCKET!, Key: event.key }));
  return { user, size: obj.ContentLength };
};
```

- **Edge cases:**
  - `/tmp` (between **512 MB and 10,240 MB**, configurable) also survives while the environment is frozen. Treat it exactly like Factor VI's "cache for one transaction": check before reuse, never rely on it.
  - A promise you didn't `await` doesn't die when the handler returns. It is **frozen and resumes during the next invocation**, so a slow analytics call from request A can run, and fail, inside request B. Await everything, or the work leaks across requests.

#### Factor V on serverless: versions and aliases are the release ledger ➕
- **Problem:** "deploy the function" with a plain code update overwrites `$LATEST` in place. Nothing records what ran before, so there is nothing to roll back to.
- **Why it matters:** this is the snowflake failure from Factor V. A mutable deploy target can't be re-run.
- **Mechanism:** **publishing a version** creates an **immutable snapshot of code and configuration**, environment variables included. That matches Factor V's definition of a release exactly: build + config, frozen, with an ever-increasing number that is never reused. An **alias** (`live`) is a movable pointer to a version. Triggers point at the alias, never at `$LATEST`. A **weighted alias** can split traffic between **two** versions, which gives you a canary.

```bash
# release = publish an immutable version (code + env vars together)
VERSION=$(aws lambda publish-version --function-name api --query Version --output text)

# canary: 5% of traffic to the new version, 95% stays on the current one
aws lambda update-alias --function-name api --name live \
  --routing-config "AdditionalVersionWeights={\"$VERSION\"=0.05}"

# promote: point the alias at the new version and clear the split
aws lambda update-alias --function-name api --name live --function-version "$VERSION" \
  --routing-config "AdditionalVersionWeights={}"

# rollback = move the pointer back; nothing is rebuilt
aws lambda update-alias --function-name api --name live --function-version 41
```

- **Edge cases:** changing an environment variable only changes `$LATEST`; published versions keep their old values. So a config change also needs a new published version, which is exactly what Factor V asks for. Secrets fetched at runtime from a secret manager are **not** part of the version, so rotating them doesn't need a release (and rolling back doesn't restore an old password). At low traffic the weighted split is probabilistic, so a 5% canary may get no requests at all for a while.

#### Factor VIII on serverless: the formation is a concurrency limit ➕
- **Problem:** there is no `replicas: 3` to write. A traffic spike or a retry storm can scale the function to hundreds of environments and overwhelm a database sized for three servers.
- **Why:** the platform owns the process model. Each concurrent event gets its own environment, so **concurrency is your process count**.
- **Mechanism:** the knob that replaces the process formation is **reserved concurrency**: a hard cap on how many environments the function may run at once. Size it from what the downstream services can take, not from what users might send.

```bash
# at most 50 environments at once: about 50 DB connections with a pool of 1 per environment
aws lambda put-function-concurrency --function-name api --reserved-concurrent-executions 50
```

- **Edge cases:** above the cap, synchronous callers get **throttling errors (HTTP 429)**, so clients need retries with backoff. Queue-triggered functions just consume more slowly, which is usually what you want. Separate process types still matter: a heavy image job and the API should be **different functions**, each with its own cap, for the same reason Factor VIII separates web from worker.

#### Factor IX on serverless: there is no SIGTERM for your code ➕
- **Problem:** the Nest app that flushed logs and metrics in `onApplicationShutdown` never runs that hook on Lambda. The last few seconds of telemetry go missing.
- **Why:** with no extensions registered, the Shutdown phase gives the runtime **0 ms**. The process is killed without calling your handler. The environment may also be frozen indefinitely and then discarded.
- **Mechanism:** use **crash-only design** fully (Factor IX's last idea): finish all work and **flush before the handler returns**. Boot cost moves into the request path on a cold start, so cache the expensive bootstrap in module scope and reuse it on warm starts.

```ts title="queue-handler.ts (NestJS context cached per environment)"
import { NestFactory } from '@nestjs/core';
import type { INestApplicationContext } from '@nestjs/common';
import { WorkerModule } from './worker.module';
import { ThumbnailService } from './thumbnail.service';

let ctx: Promise<INestApplicationContext> | undefined;        // bootstrapped once per environment
const getCtx = () => (ctx ??= NestFactory.createApplicationContext(WorkerModule, { bufferLogs: true }));

export const handler = async (event: { Records: { body: string }[] }) => {
  const app = await getCtx();
  const svc = app.get(ThumbnailService);
  for (const r of event.Records) await svc.process(JSON.parse(r.body));   // awaited: nothing leaks to the next event
  // flush buffered telemetry here, before returning: no shutdown hook will run
};
```

- **Edge cases:** if `Init` exceeds 10 seconds, Lambda retries initialization during the first invocation under the function timeout, so a heavy Nest module graph shows up as an occasional slow first request. A function that crashes or times out has its environment **reset**, so the next event pays a cold start.

#### Factor XII on serverless: admin tasks are their own invocation ➕
- **Problem:** there is no shell on a function to run `npm run migration:run` in.
- **Mechanism:** ship the admin task in the **same artifact** and invoke it as a separate function (or a one-off container task) pinned to the **same version** as the release, then wait for it to finish.

```bash
# same code + config as release 42, run once, wait for the result (synchronous)
aws lambda invoke --function-name api-admin:42 --invocation-type RequestResponse \
  --cli-binary-format raw-in-base64-out --payload '{"task":"migrate"}' out.json && cat out.json
```

- **Edge cases:** a Lambda invocation stops at the **15-minute** timeout, so long backfills must be chunked (section 17). **Asynchronous** invocations are retried automatically on error, so an admin task invoked asynchronously can run more than once: invoke synchronously, and make it idempotent anyway.

## 11. Factor VIII: scale out via the process model 🎓

### The 2011 problem
Every runtime scaled its own way:
- **Java:** one huge JVM process grabbed a big block of RAM at startup and **multiplexed with threads** inside it.
- **PHP:** Apache spawned **small child processes on demand**.

### What it says
Make the **process a first-class citizen**. Design the app from the start to scale as **more processes**, modelled on Unix service daemons. Split work into **process types**:
- **web**: handles HTTP requests
- **worker**: heavy background jobs (video encoding, image resizing)
- **scheduler**: timed jobs (an email to every user at midnight)

Each type **scales independently by running more copies.**

### The lesson's food-delivery numbers 🎓
Each process uses roughly **500 MB**.

| Time | Need | One combined process type | Split process types |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lunch | 10 web, 2 workers | scale the combined process to 10 → **10 × 500 MB = 5 GB** web + **5 GB** of idle worker capacity = **10 GB** | 10 web + 2 workers = **6 GB** |
| Night | 5 web, 20 workers | must scale everything to 20 | 5 web + 20 workers |

Scaling the web tier shouldn't force you to scale workers you don't need.

### Process formation 🎓
The **set of process types and counts** (web=3, worker=2) is the **process formation**. You still write it today: in a **Procfile**, or as `replicas: 3` in a Kubernetes Deployment.

```text title="Procfile"
web: node dist/main.js
worker: node dist/worker.js
scheduler: node dist/scheduler.js
```

```yaml title="🔧 the same formation in Kubernetes (one Deployment per process type)"
apiVersion: apps/v1
kind: Deployment
metadata: { name: api-worker }
spec:
  replicas: 20                       # night formation for workers
  selector: { matchLabels: { app: api, proc: worker } }
  template:
    metadata: { labels: { app: api, proc: worker } }
    spec:
      containers:
        - name: worker
          image: 123456789012.dkr.ecr.eu-west-1.amazonaws.com/api@sha256:cd34...   # same image as web
          command: ["node", "dist/worker.js"]
```

### Two notes 🎓
1. **Threads are still allowed inside a process**: JVM threads, goroutines, Node's event loop. But the **unit of scaling is the process**, because one machine can only grow so far (the vertical limit), while share-nothing processes spread across many machines without a second thought.
2. **The app never daemonizes and never writes PID files.** Managing a process's lifecycle is the **platform's job** (the OS init system, Kubernetes, a process manager). The app runs in the **foreground** and does its work.

### Status today 🎓
**Orchestrators** run most of the internet: you declare a formation and the platform keeps it true by starting, killing, restarting and moving containers. The factor didn't become outdated; **it was adopted so completely you rarely think about it**, unless you deploy straight onto an OS with **systemd**, where it still applies directly.

## 12. Schedulers and workers inside the formation ➕

Factor VIII lists a `scheduler` and a `worker` process type, and Factor VI warned that in-process cron runs once per instance; what neither says is how to make "run this once" true when the platform runs copies of everything and replaces them whenever it likes.

The queue-side fix (a scheduler stored in Redis that emits one job per tick) is in [Task Queues and Background Jobs](/docs/backend-first-principles/14-task-queues-background-jobs). This section is about the **process formation** side.

#### The singleton problem: `replicas: 1` is not "exactly one" ➕
- **Problem:** you split the scheduler into its own process type with `replicas: 1`, so the midnight billing job should run once. One night it runs twice and customers are charged twice.
- **Why it happens (first principles):** an orchestrator promises to converge on **the count you asked for**, not that the count is never exceeded.
  - During a **rolling update**, `maxSurge` starts the new scheduler **before** the old one stops. For a few seconds there are two, and if a tick falls in that window, both fire.
  - When a **node stops responding**, the platform can't tell a dead machine from a cut network. It starts a replacement elsewhere while the old process may still be running.
- **Mechanism:** stop relying on the process count. Make **each tick claimable exactly once in the database**, so a second scheduler that fires the same tick loses the race and does nothing. A unique key on `(job, scheduled_for)` is the simplest fence.

```sql
CREATE TABLE scheduled_runs (
  job           text        NOT NULL,
  scheduled_for timestamptz NOT NULL,
  claimed_by    text        NOT NULL,
  claimed_at    timestamptz NOT NULL DEFAULT now(),
  PRIMARY KEY (job, scheduled_for)              -- the fence: one row per tick, ever
);
```

```ts title="src/scheduler/tick.ts"
export async function runOnce(db: DataSource, job: string, tick: Date, work: () => Promise<void>) {
  const rows = await db.query(
    `INSERT INTO scheduled_runs (job, scheduled_for, claimed_by)
     VALUES ($1, $2, $3) ON CONFLICT DO NOTHING RETURNING job`,
    [job, tick.toISOString(), process.env.HOSTNAME ?? 'unknown'],
  );
  if (rows.length === 0) return;                 // another scheduler already claimed this tick
  await work();                                   // the work itself must still be idempotent (see below)
}

// tick = the scheduled time, truncated (e.g. midnight UTC), NOT "now": both copies must compute the same key
```

- **Edge cases:**
  - Claiming the tick and doing the work aren't atomic. If the process dies after the insert, the tick is claimed but not done. Record a `finished_at`, and have a watchdog alert on (or re-run) claims that never finished. That re-run is only safe if the work is idempotent, which is Factor IX's re-entrancy rule again.
  - A Postgres **advisory lock** (`pg_try_advisory_lock`) is the other common fence, but a session-level lock belongs to **one connection**. Through a connection pool, the lock may be taken on one connection and the work run on another, and the lock disappears silently if that connection is recycled. Prefer the row claim, or `pg_try_advisory_xact_lock` inside one transaction.
  - Setting `strategy: Recreate` on the scheduler Deployment removes the surge overlap, but not the node-partition case. Use it in addition to the fence, not instead of it.

#### Kubernetes CronJob: let the platform be the scheduler ➕
- **Problem:** a long-running scheduler process is one more thing to keep alive, monitor and fence, just to wake up once a night.
- **Mechanism:** a **CronJob** creates a **Job** on each tick, and the Job runs a container that does the work and **exits**. It is Factor XII's one-off admin process on a timer: same image, same config, a different command.

```yaml title="k8s/billing-cronjob.yaml"
apiVersion: batch/v1
kind: CronJob
metadata: { name: nightly-billing }
spec:
  schedule: "0 0 * * *"
  timeZone: "Etc/UTC"                 # explicit, so a cluster setting can't move the run
  concurrencyPolicy: Forbid           # if last night's run is still going, skip this one
  startingDeadlineSeconds: 600        # missed by more than 10 min (controller down)? skip, don't run late
  successfulJobsHistoryLimit: 3
  failedJobsHistoryLimit: 5
  jobTemplate:
    spec:
      backoffLimit: 2
      activeDeadlineSeconds: 3600     # a hung run is killed after an hour
      template:
        spec:
          restartPolicy: Never
          containers:
            - name: billing
              image: 123456789012.dkr.ecr.eu-west-1.amazonaws.com/api@sha256:cd34...   # same image as web
              command: ["node", "dist/tasks/nightly-billing.js"]
              envFrom: [{ secretRef: { name: api-config } }]
```

- **Edge cases:**
  - The Kubernetes docs say a CronJob creates a Job **about once** per schedule time: in some situations it creates **two**, or none. `concurrencyPolicy: Forbid` stops overlap, not duplicates of the same tick. Keep the database fence from the previous subsection inside the task.
  - If the controller misses more than **100** schedules (for example a CronJob suspended for a long time with no `startingDeadlineSeconds`), it stops scheduling and logs an error. Setting a deadline avoids this.
  - The task must **exit** with a meaningful code. A process that keeps an open Redis handle and never exits leaves the Job running until `activeDeadlineSeconds` kills it.

#### Workers: scale on backlog, not CPU ➕
- **Problem:** the email queue has 40,000 waiting jobs, but the autoscaler won't add workers.
- **Why:** workers that call an external API spend their time **waiting on the network**, so CPU stays low while the backlog grows. CPU measures how busy a process is, not how much work is waiting. For workers, the **queue depth** is the real load signal.
- **Mechanism:** scale the worker process type on the backlog. **KEDA** is a Kubernetes add-on that reads an external metric (here, the length of a Redis list) and drives the replica count from it, including down to zero when the queue is empty.

```yaml title="k8s/email-worker-scaler.yaml (KEDA)"
apiVersion: keda.sh/v1alpha1
kind: ScaledObject
metadata: { name: email-worker }
spec:
  scaleTargetRef: { name: api-worker }        # the worker Deployment from Factor VIII
  minReplicaCount: 1
  maxReplicaCount: 30
  triggers:
    - type: redis
      metadata:
        address: redis.internal:6379
        listName: bull:email:wait             # BullMQ keeps waiting jobs in this list
        listLength: "500"                     # target about 500 waiting jobs per worker
```

- **Edge cases:**
  - Scaling **down** stops workers mid-job. Set `terminationGracePeriodSeconds` longer than a typical job so the shutdown drain (Factor IX) can finish, and keep jobs re-entrant for the ones that can't.
  - Cap `maxReplicaCount` by what the **downstream** can take. 30 workers × 10 concurrent jobs = 300 parallel calls to an email provider that may rate-limit at 100.
  - With `minReplicaCount: 0`, the first job after an idle period waits for a pod to start: fine for email, wrong for a job the user is waiting on.

## 13. Factor IX: maximize robustness with fast startup and graceful shutdown 🎓

### What it says
Processes are **disposable**: they can be **started or stopped at a moment's notice**, and neither event does damage. (Graceful shutdown got its own deep dive in an earlier lesson; this is the summary.)

### Why disposability matters: stops and starts are routine 🎓
- Scaling out starts processes; deploys kill old ones and start new ones from the new image; the orchestrator moves processes between machines whenever it likes.
- **Deploy 5 times a week across 10 instances = 50 stops and 50 starts**, before any autoscaling.
- **A process dying is part of the release cycle, not an incident.**

### Fast startup 🎓
If boot takes **60 seconds**, a rolling deploy of **10 instances takes 10 minutes**, and an autoscaler reacts to a spike **a minute late**. At **2 seconds**, both are negligible. **Boot should take seconds.**

### Graceful shutdown for a web process 🎓
On **SIGTERM**: **stop accepting new requests → finish in-flight requests → exit.** Skip that and every deploy throws errors at the requests that were mid-flight.

<Mermaid chart={`sequenceDiagram
  autonumber
  participant O as Orchestrator
  participant LB as Load balancer
  participant W as Web process
  O->>W: SIGTERM (grace period starts, often 30 s)
  O->>LB: remove instance from targets
  W->>W: stop accepting new connections
  W->>W: finish the 5 in-flight requests
  W->>W: close DB pool, flush logs
  W-->>O: exit 0
  Note over O,W: still running when the grace period ends: SIGKILL`} />

### Graceful shutdown for a worker 🎓
- **Return the current job to the queue** instead of discarding it.
- That only works if jobs are **re-entrant**: an interrupted job must leave **no dirty state**, so running it again is safe.
- Two ways to get there: **wrap the whole job in a transaction**, or make it **idempotent** (running it twice gives the same result; a duplicate is detected and skipped).

### Robust against sudden death 🎓
SIGTERM with about **30 seconds** to wrap up is the *polite* case. Hardware fails; a container exceeding its memory limit is **OOM-killed with no warning**. Design so **any process can die mid-instruction**. The document points to **crash-only design**: if the app survives crashes, it barely needs a separate graceful path, because **the crash path is the shutdown path**.

### 🔧 Implementation (NestJS + a queue worker)

```ts title="src/main.ts"
app.enableShutdownHooks();       // Nest listens for SIGTERM/SIGINT and runs onModuleDestroy / onApplicationShutdown
```

```ts title="src/worker/thumbnail.processor.ts"
// Idempotent job: safe to run twice, safe to be killed halfway
async process(job: { id: string; fileId: string }) {
  await this.db.transaction(async (tx) => {
    const claimed = await tx.query(
      `UPDATE files SET thumb_status = 'PROCESSING', thumb_job = $2
       WHERE id = $1 AND thumb_status IN ('PENDING', 'FAILED') RETURNING id`,
      [job.fileId, job.id],
    );
    if (claimed.length === 0) return;                    // already done or claimed: a duplicate, skip
    const key = `thumbs/${job.fileId}/256.webp`;           // deterministic key: a re-run overwrites, never duplicates
    await this.images.renderAndUpload(job.fileId, key);
    await tx.query(`UPDATE files SET thumb_status = 'DONE', thumb_key = $2 WHERE id = $1`, [job.fileId, key]);
  });
}

async onApplicationShutdown() {
  await this.worker.close();     // stop taking jobs, let the current one finish (or it returns to the queue)
}
```

🔧 **Edge cases:**
- **PID 1 and signals:** with a shell-form `CMD npm start`, SIGTERM goes to `sh` or `npm`, which may not forward it, so Node never shuts down gracefully and gets SIGKILLed. Use exec form `CMD ["node", "dist/main.js"]`, or run with an init (`docker run --init`).
- **Kubernetes race:** SIGTERM and removal from Service endpoints happen in parallel, so a few requests can still arrive after SIGTERM. A short `preStop` sleep before shutting down closes that gap.
- Keep the in-flight drain **shorter than the grace period**, or the platform SIGKILLs you anyway.

## 14. Factor X: keep development, staging and production as similar as possible 🎓

Known as **dev/prod parity** (parity = sameness).

### The three gaps
1. **Time gap:** code written today reaches production on Friday or next week, alongside lots of other merged code, so many changes land at once.
2. **Personnel gap:** the person who wrote the code isn't the one who deploys it, and neither is the ops person who sees the error.
3. **Tools gap:** a MacBook or Windows laptop with its own stack (native installs or Docker) versus a **Linux** server talking to external backing services. Different architecture, and the difference causes bugs.

| Gap | Traditional app | Twelve-factor app |
|---|---|---|
| Time | weeks between deploys | hours |
| Personnel | authors and deployers differ | authors deploy and read the logs |
| Tools | different stacks per environment | as similar as possible |

Shrink all three and continuous deployment becomes smooth. Time and personnel are largely closed today. **The tools gap persists.**

### The SQLite-in-dev trap 🎓
ORMs (Drizzle, Prisma in Node; GORM in Go; SQLAlchemy in Python) abstract the database, which tempts people to use **SQLite locally and Postgres in production**.

- In **Postgres**, `LIKE` is **case-sensitive** and `ILIKE` is the case-insensitive version.
- In **SQLite**, `LIKE` is **case-insensitive by default for ASCII**.
- A search feature built and tested locally behaves differently in production. **No error, no warning, no log.** Behaviour just changes.

```sql
-- Postgres
SELECT 'Backend' LIKE 'back%';    -- false
SELECT 'Backend' ILIKE 'back%';   -- true
-- SQLite
SELECT 'Backend' LIKE 'back%';    -- 1 (true)
```

**The advice:** use **the same backing services** (database, cache, queue) in local, staging and production. In 2011 that was hard; today it's **one command** to start a container.

```yaml title="docker-compose.yml (🔧 match production's engines and major versions)"
services:
  db:
    image: postgres:16.4          # same major as the RDS instance
    environment: { POSTGRES_USER: app, POSTGRES_PASSWORD: app, POSTGRES_DB: app }
    ports: ["5432:5432"]
  redis:
    image: redis:7.4
    ports: ["6379:6379"]
  s3:
    image: minio/minio:RELEASE.2024-12-18T13-15-44Z
    command: server /data
    ports: ["9000:9000"]
```

### 2026 nuance: managed services reopen the gap 🎓
For a while Docker and Compose made local and production nearly identical. Then teams moved to **managed services**. **DynamoDB** can't run locally; emulators such as **LocalStack** imitate documented behaviour, but the real service is proprietary, so they aren't the same implementation. The lesson doesn't say "avoid managed services": they bring big scaling and cost benefits. **If the benefit is large, accept the parity gap knowingly.**

🔧 Close what remains of the gap with **integration tests against a real cloud staging environment** for the managed pieces, and **Testcontainers** (a real Postgres in CI) for the rest.

## 15. Factor XI: treat logs as event streams 🎓

### The idea
Historically a log meant **a log file**. The factor reframes it: **the file was never the log; it was one output format.** A log is a **time-ordered stream of events** that flows for as long as the app runs, with no fixed start or end.

### What it says
- The app **does not route or store its log stream**: no file paths, no log rotation.
- It writes every event, **unbuffered, to stdout**.
- The **execution environment** captures the stream and decides where it goes: a file, or a **log drain** (a backing service for analysis and storage).

### Why 🎓
1. **Logging stops being the app's concern.** Moving to a new environment changes config, not code. In 2011 stdout went to **syslog**; today it goes to **Loki** or **Datadog**. Same pattern.
2. **A stream composes; a file doesn't.**
   - **Laptop:** you read the stream live in your terminal.
   - **Container:** the runtime captures it, stamps which process it came from, and hands it to a collector.
   - **Production:** the platform merges the streams of all instances into **one time-ordered stream**, so you search by time instead of hunting through each instance.

<Mermaid chart={`flowchart LR
  A1["instance 1<br/>stdout"] --> CR["container runtime<br/>captures + labels"]
  A2["instance 2<br/>stdout"] --> CR
  A3["instance 3<br/>stdout"] --> CR
  CR --> AG["collector / agent"]
  AG --> L["Loki / Datadog / CloudWatch<br/>one merged, time-ordered stream"]`} />

### Observability is an addition 🎓
Today's **observability** is **logs, metrics and traces**. The 2011 text only covers logs because the other two weren't common practice yet. Treat metrics and traces as additions; the factor doesn't forbid them.

### 🔧 Implementation: structured JSON to stdout

```ts title="src/main.ts"
import { Logger } from 'nestjs-pino';
const app = await NestFactory.create(AppModule, { bufferLogs: true });
app.useLogger(app.get(Logger));
```

```ts title="src/app.module.ts"
LoggerModule.forRoot({
  pinoHttp: {
    level: process.env.LOG_LEVEL ?? 'info',          // config, not code
    // no transport, no file destination: JSON lines go to stdout
    redact: ['req.headers.authorization', 'req.headers.cookie'],
    customProps: () => ({ commit: process.env.GIT_SHA }),
  },
}),
```

🔧 **Edge cases:** pretty-printing belongs in local dev only (`pino-pretty` piped in the terminal), not in production. Never log secrets or full request bodies. On `process.exit()` asynchronous log writes can be lost, so flush before exiting.

## 16. Factor XII: run admin and management tasks as one-off processes 🎓

### What a one-off process is
A task that runs **once, next to the long-running processes, then exits**: a **database migration**, a script to **fix data in production**.

### What it says
A one-off admin process runs in an **identical environment** to the app's regular processes:
- **same codebase, same commit** (so a deploy ships both together),
- **same config**,
- **same dependency isolation**.

### Why so strict 🎓
In the old days, fixing a record meant **SSH into the live server and run an `UPDATE` or `DELETE` by hand**. No code review, no record, no log of what was done. Production drifted from what the code and docs describe.

**The best-known example:** **database migrations** ship with the code and run as a one-off process before the new version starts, then exit.

### 🔧 Implementation: same image, different command

```json title="package.json scripts"
{
  "scripts": {
    "migration:run": "typeorm migration:run -d dist/db/data-source.js",
    "task:archive-old": "node dist/tasks/archive-old.js"
  }
}
```

```bash
# AWS ECS: one-off task from the SAME task definition revision (same image digest, same config)
aws ecs run-task --cluster prod --task-definition api:102 --launch-type FARGATE \
  --network-configuration 'awsvpcConfiguration={subnets=[subnet-abc],securityGroups=[sg-123]}' \
  --overrides '{"containerOverrides":[{"name":"api","command":["npm","run","migration:run"]}]}'
```

```yaml title="🔧 Kubernetes: a Job using the release's image"

kind: Job

  backoffLimit: 0                 # a failed migration should stop the deploy, not retry blindly
```

🔧 **Edge cases:** two migration runners started at once (one per instance) can race. Run migrations as **one** job before the rollout, or rely on the tool's lock (TypeORM and Prisma take an advisory lock on Postgres). Data-fix scripts go through code review like any other change.

## 17. Running migrations safely as one-off processes ➕

Factor XII says a migration is a one-off process from the same release; it doesn't say what makes that process safe to run against a database that is serving live traffic, which is where most migration outages actually come from.

Migration files and tools are covered in [Databases (Postgres)](/docs/backend-first-principles/12-databases-postgres). The order of schema changes relative to the app rollout (expand, then contract) is covered in [DevOps](/docs/backend-first-principles/31-devops). This section is about **executing** the one-off.

#### Exactly one runner, before the rollout, and a failure stops the deploy ➕
- **Problem:** the easiest setup runs migrations **at app boot** (TypeORM's `migrationsRun: true`, or `prisma migrate deploy` in the start script). It works with one instance, then breaks as you grow.
- **Why it breaks:**
  - **Ten replicas start at once, so ten runners race.** The tool's lock usually serialises them, but nine instances sit blocked at boot.
  - **Boot is no longer fast** (Factor IX). A 4-minute migration means every new pod takes 4 minutes to become ready, and a liveness or startup probe may **kill the pod halfway through the migration**.
  - **Web and migration permissions are mixed.** The web process now needs DDL rights (`CREATE`, `ALTER`, `DROP`) it should never have at runtime.
- **Mechanism:** run the migration as **its own step** in the pipeline, as one process from the release's image. The pipeline **waits for it to succeed** before it starts the rollout, and a failure stops the deploy. Give it a separate database role with DDL rights; the web role gets only DML.

```bash
# from the Kubernetes Job in the previous section (backoffLimit: 0)
kubectl apply -f migrate-cd34.yaml
kubectl wait --for=condition=complete job/migrate-cd34 --timeout=15m \
  || { kubectl logs job/migrate-cd34; exit 1; }          # failed or timed out: no rollout
kubectl set image deployment/api api=123456789012.dkr.ecr.eu-west-1.amazonaws.com/api@sha256:cd34...
```

- **Edge cases:** `kubectl wait --for=condition=complete` doesn't return early when the Job **fails**; it waits for the full timeout. Keep the timeout realistic, or poll for both `complete` and `failed`. Keep the tool's advisory lock as a backstop in case two pipelines run at once. A failed migration that contained non-transactional statements (next subsections) may leave partial work behind, so the next run must cope with it.

#### The lock queue: why a 1 ms `ALTER TABLE` can take the site down ➕
- **Problem:** a migration adds a nullable column. It should take milliseconds. Instead, every request touching `orders` hangs for two minutes and the API returns 504s.
- **Why it happens (first principles):**
  1. Most `ALTER TABLE` forms need an **`ACCESS EXCLUSIVE`** lock, which conflicts with **every** other lock, including the light lock a plain `SELECT` takes.
  2. A long analytics query is **already reading** `orders`. The `ALTER` must wait for it to finish.
  3. Postgres grants locks in queue order. Every new `SELECT` on `orders` now queues **behind the waiting `ALTER`**, even though the `SELECT` doesn't conflict with the analytics query.
  4. Result: one slow query + one tiny migration = the table is unusable until the slow query ends.

<Mermaid chart={`sequenceDiagram
  autonumber
  participant R as Report query
  participant M as Migration (ALTER)
  participant A as API requests
  participant T as orders table
  R->>T: SELECT running for 2 min (light lock held)
  M->>T: ALTER needs ACCESS EXCLUSIVE, waits behind R
  A->>T: SELECT ... queued behind M
  A->>T: SELECT ... queued behind M
  Note over A,T: API times out although ALTER itself takes 1 ms
  R-->>T: report finishes
  M-->>T: ALTER runs, queue drains`} />

- **Mechanism:** never let a migration **wait** for a lock. Set **`lock_timeout`**, which aborts a statement that waits longer than the limit **for a lock** (only the waiting, not the work). The migration fails fast, the queued API requests continue, and you **retry** a few seconds later. Add **`statement_timeout`** as a ceiling on the work itself.

```sql title="db/migrations/20261002_add_orders_note.sql (dbmate)"
-- migrate:up
-- dbmate wraps this file in a transaction, so SET LOCAL ends with it
SET LOCAL lock_timeout = '3s';          -- give up on the lock after 3 s instead of blocking traffic
SET LOCAL statement_timeout = '60s';    -- and never run longer than a minute
ALTER TABLE orders ADD COLUMN note text;

-- migrate:down
ALTER TABLE orders DROP COLUMN note;
```

```bash title="scripts/migrate-with-retry.sh"
for attempt in 1 2 3 4 5; do
  dbmate up && exit 0
  echo "migration failed (attempt $attempt), probably lock_timeout, retrying in 10s"
  sleep 10
done
exit 1
```

- **Edge cases:**
  - Inside a transaction, `SET LOCAL` scopes the setting to that transaction. A plain `SET` lasts for the session, which matters when a pool hands the connection to someone else afterwards.
  - Before a risky migration, look for long or idle-in-transaction sessions: `SELECT pid, state, now() - xact_start AS age, query FROM pg_stat_activity WHERE xact_start IS NOT NULL ORDER BY age DESC;`. A forgotten `BEGIN` in someone's SQL client blocks DDL just as well as a slow report.
  - Retrying works only if the migration is **transactional** (Postgres runs DDL in a transaction), so a timed-out attempt leaves nothing behind.

#### DDL that doesn't block: the safe forms ➕
- **Problem:** some schema changes don't just wait for a lock; they **hold** a heavy lock while scanning or rewriting the whole table. On a 50-million-row table, that means minutes of blocked writes.
- **Why:** to add a constraint, Postgres must prove every existing row satisfies it, which means a full scan under a lock. Some column changes force a **rewrite** of every row.
- **Mechanism:** each dangerous operation has a form that splits the expensive part from the locking part:

| Change | Blocking form | Non-blocking form |
|---|---|---|
| Add an index | `CREATE INDEX` (blocks writes during the build) | `CREATE INDEX CONCURRENTLY` (reads and writes continue) |
| Add a foreign key or CHECK | `ADD CONSTRAINT` (scans the table under a heavy lock) | `ADD CONSTRAINT ... NOT VALID`, then `VALIDATE CONSTRAINT` (validation takes only `SHARE UPDATE EXCLUSIVE`) |
| Make a column NOT NULL | `SET NOT NULL` (full scan under `ACCESS EXCLUSIVE`) | add `CHECK (col IS NOT NULL) NOT VALID`, validate it, then `SET NOT NULL` (Postgres skips the scan when a valid CHECK proves it) |
| Add a column with a default | volatile default such as `clock_timestamp()` (rewrites every row) | non-volatile default (stored in metadata, no rewrite) |
| Rename or drop a column in use | one migration | expand and contract over several releases ([DevOps](/docs/backend-first-principles/31-devops)) |

```sql title="db/migrations/20261003_orders_customer_fk.sql (dbmate)"
-- migrate:up transaction:false
-- CONCURRENTLY cannot run inside a transaction block, so this file opts out
-- no transaction here, so a plain SET (SET LOCAL would have no effect)
SET lock_timeout = '3s';
CREATE INDEX CONCURRENTLY IF NOT EXISTS orders_customer_id_idx ON orders (customer_id);
ALTER TABLE orders ADD CONSTRAINT orders_customer_fk
  FOREIGN KEY (customer_id) REFERENCES customers (id) NOT VALID;   -- instant: no scan
ALTER TABLE orders VALIDATE CONSTRAINT orders_customer_fk;          -- scans, but writes continue

-- migrate:down transaction:false
ALTER TABLE orders DROP CONSTRAINT IF EXISTS orders_customer_fk;
DROP INDEX CONCURRENTLY IF EXISTS orders_customer_id_idx;
```

- **Edge cases:**
  - If `CREATE INDEX CONCURRENTLY` fails (deadlock, a uniqueness violation), it leaves an **`INVALID` index** behind: ignored by queries but still updated on every write. `IF NOT EXISTS` would then skip it on retry and keep the broken index. Drop it and build again, or use `REINDEX INDEX CONCURRENTLY`.
  - Because this file runs **outside a transaction**, a failure halfway leaves the earlier statements applied. Keep one non-transactional operation per file when you can, and write each step so it can be re-run.
  - `NOT VALID` constraints are still enforced for **new** rows immediately. Only the check of old rows is postponed.

#### Backfills are a separate one-off process, in batches ➕
- **Problem:** the new column needs values for 50 million existing rows. `UPDATE orders SET region = ...` in the migration runs for an hour, holds row locks on everything it touched, generates a mountain of WAL, makes read replicas fall behind, and can't be stopped without losing all the work done so far.
- **Why:** one statement is one transaction. Postgres can't commit half of it, so the cost of being interrupted is everything.
- **Mechanism:** keep **schema** changes in the migration (fast, transactional) and move **data** changes to a separate admin process (Factor XII) that works in **small, committed batches**. It is **resumable**, because it picks up from rows not yet done, and **idempotent**, because a re-run of a batch writes the same values.

```ts title="src/tasks/backfill-order-region.ts (run as a one-off, same image)"
import { dataSource } from '../db/data-source';

const BATCH = 5_000;
const PAUSE_MS = 200;                                    // breathing room for replicas and live traffic

async function main() {
  await dataSource.initialize();
  let lastId = process.env.START_ID ?? '0';              // resume point: the last id logged by a previous run
  for (;;) {
    // 1. find the next id range by primary key (cheap, uses the PK index)
    const batch: { id: string }[] = await dataSource.query(
      `SELECT id FROM orders WHERE id > $1 ORDER BY id LIMIT $2`, [lastId, BATCH]);
    if (batch.length === 0) break;
    const maxId = batch[batch.length - 1].id;

    // 2. fill only rows in that range that are still empty: re-running a range is harmless
    const res = await dataSource.query(
      `UPDATE orders o SET region = c.region
       FROM customers c
       WHERE o.customer_id = c.id AND o.id > $1 AND o.id <= $2 AND o.region IS NULL`,
      [lastId, maxId]);

    lastId = maxId;
    console.log(JSON.stringify({ msg: 'backfill batch', lastId, updated: res[1] }));
    await new Promise((r) => setTimeout(r, PAUSE_MS));
  }
  await dataSource.destroy();
}

main().catch((err) => { console.error(err); process.exit(1); });
```

- **Edge cases:**
  - Run the backfill **after** the release that writes the new column for new rows. Otherwise rows inserted during the backfill stay empty.
  - Watch **replication lag** while it runs and increase the pause if replicas fall behind. Readers on replicas would otherwise see stale data.
  - The loop advances by **primary-key range**, not by "rows still `NULL`". Rows whose customer has no region legitimately stay `NULL`; a loop that kept selecting `WHERE region IS NULL LIMIT 5000` would pick them up again forever, and a batch where nothing matched would end the loop early.
  - With TypeORM on Postgres, `query()` on an `UPDATE` returns the rows and the affected count as a pair, which is why the log reads `res[1]`. Check your driver's return shape before trusting the count.
  - Only after the backfill finishes and is verified can a later migration add the `NOT NULL` constraint (using the safe form above).

## 20. What the twelve factors leave out ➕

The scorecard grades what the document says; this section covers what it never said, because three gaps matter as soon as your service is one of many talking to each other.

In **2016**, Kevin Hoffman's O'Reilly book **Beyond the Twelve-Factor App** revisited the list for cloud-native systems and ended with **fifteen** factors. It reorders and reworks the originals and adds three: **API first**, **telemetry**, and **authentication and authorization**. They aren't in the classic 12factor.net text, but they fill real holes in the contract.

<Mermaid chart={`flowchart LR
  TF["Twelve factors<br/>app to platform contract"] --> G1["API first<br/>app to other apps contract"]
  TF --> G2["Telemetry<br/>app to operators contract"]
  TF --> G3["AuthN and AuthZ<br/>who may call whom"]
  G3 --> WI["Workload identity<br/>no static credentials"]`} />

#### API first: the contract with other apps comes before the code ➕
- **Problem:** the twelve factors define the contract between an app and its **platform**. They say nothing about the contract between an app and its **consumers**: the frontend, the mobile app, three other services. In practice consumers build against whatever the backend happens to return, and a renamed field is discovered by a crash in production.
- **Why it happens:** with code-first development, the interface is a side effect of the implementation, so any refactor can change it without anyone deciding to.
- **Mechanism:** make the API description (OpenAPI for HTTP) a **reviewed artifact that comes first**, and make CI **refuse breaking changes** to it, the way Factor II makes the dependency list an explicit artifact. How to write and use the description is in [OpenAPI](/docs/backend-first-principles/29-openapi). The twelve-factor-style addition is the **gate** in the pipeline:

```yaml title=".github/workflows/api-contract.yml"
name: api-contract
on: pull_request
permissions: { contents: read }
jobs:
  breaking-changes:
    runs-on: ubuntu-latest
    steps:
      - uses: actions/checkout@v4
        with: { fetch-depth: 0 }
      - name: Spec on main vs spec in this PR
        run: git show origin/main:openapi.yaml > base.yaml
      - name: Fail on changes that break existing clients
        run: |
          docker run --rm -v "$PWD:/specs" tufin/oasdiff \
            breaking /specs/base.yaml /specs/openapi.yaml --fail-on ERR
```

- **Edge cases:** a spec **generated from code** still works with this gate, as long as the generated file is committed and diffed. A deliberate breaking change needs a new version or route next to the old one, plus a deprecation period, not an override of the check. The gate only covers what the spec describes: error shapes and headers left out of the spec can still break silently.

#### Telemetry: being observable is part of the contract ➕
- **Problem:** Factor XI covers logs only. An app can follow all twelve factors and still be a black box: no metrics to autoscale on, no traces to find which of five services is slow.
- **Mechanism (the twelve-factor angle):** apply Factor XI's principle to all telemetry. The app **emits** metrics and traces in a **standard protocol** (OTLP, from OpenTelemetry), and **where they go is config**, set by the platform, never hard-coded. What to measure and how is covered in [Logging, Monitoring and Observability](/docs/backend-first-principles/18-logging-monitoring-observability).

```bash
# the destination is deploy config, exactly like a backing-service handle
OTEL_SERVICE_NAME=api
OTEL_EXPORTER_OTLP_ENDPOINT=http://otel-collector.observability:4318
OTEL_RESOURCE_ATTRIBUTES=deployment.environment=production,service.version=cd34
```

- **Edge cases:** if the collector is down, telemetry must be **dropped**, never block requests. Exporters buffer and batch, so flush them during graceful shutdown ([Graceful Shutdown](/docs/backend-first-principles/19-graceful-shutdown)), and before the handler returns on serverless (section 10).

#### Authentication and authorization: every caller proves who it is ➕
- **Problem:** inside a private network, services often trust any request that reaches them: "it came from inside the VPC, so it's ours". One compromised pod can then call every internal API.
- **Why it happens:** the network location is treated as an identity. But network location is shared by everything in that network.
- **Mechanism:** Hoffman's factor says **every request is authenticated and authorized**, internal ones included. The service checks a token (a JWT from your identity provider, or mutual TLS certificates issued by a service mesh) and authorizes the action, the same way it does for users (see [Authentication and Authorization](/docs/backend-first-principles/08-authentication-authorization)). The same principle applies to how the app reaches its **backing services**, which is the next subsection.
- **Edge cases:** validate the token's **audience** (`aud`), not just its signature. Otherwise a token minted for service A is accepted by service B. Keep tokens short-lived so a leaked one expires on its own.

#### Workload identity: backing services without static credentials ➕
- **Problem:** Factor III puts the database password in config and Factor IV in the resource handle. Either way it is a **long-lived secret**: copied into every environment, readable by anyone who can read the config, and painful to rotate.
- **Why it happens:** the app has no identity of its own, so it borrows one by holding a shared secret.
- **Mechanism:** the **platform gives each process an identity**, and the process trades it for **short-lived credentials** on demand. It is the same idea as OIDC for CI pipelines, applied to running workloads.
  - On AWS: an ECS **task role**, a Lambda **execution role**, or on EKS a **Pod Identity** association that maps an IAM role to a Kubernetes **service account**. The AWS SDK's default credential chain finds the temporary credentials automatically: no keys in env vars.
  - For the database: **RDS IAM authentication**. Instead of a password, the app signs a token with its role. Each token is valid for **15 minutes** and only for opening the connection; an established connection keeps working after the token expires.

```ts title="src/db/pool.ts (password = a fresh IAM token per new connection)"
import { Pool } from 'pg';
import { Signer } from '@aws-sdk/rds-signer';

const signer = new Signer({
  hostname: process.env.DB_HOST!,         // the instance endpoint (a custom DNS name won't work for signing)
  port: 5432,
  username: 'api_iam',                    // a DB user granted the rds_iam role
  region: process.env.AWS_REGION,
});

export const pool = new Pool({
  host: process.env.DB_HOST,
  port: 5432,
  user: 'api_iam',
  database: 'orders',
  password: () => signer.getAuthToken(),  // called for each new connection, so tokens never go stale
  ssl: { rejectUnauthorized: true },      // IAM auth requires TLS
  max: 10,
});
```

```sql
-- one-time setup in Postgres: this user may only log in with IAM tokens
CREATE USER api_iam;
GRANT rds_iam TO api_iam;
GRANT SELECT, INSERT, UPDATE, DELETE ON ALL TABLES IN SCHEMA public TO api_iam;   -- DML only, no DDL
```

- **Edge cases:**
  - Once a Postgres user has `rds_iam`, it **must** log in with IAM; its password no longer works. Use a separate user for migrations if those run elsewhere.
  - RDS documents that IAM authentication needs extra database memory (roughly 300 to 1,000 MiB), so check headroom on small instances.
  - On EKS, block pods from the node's **instance metadata service**. Otherwise a pod can fall back to the **node's** role, which is usually broader than its own.
  - The token is generated from the app's temporary credentials, which must still be valid when the connection is opened. Long-lived processes rely on the SDK to refresh them, so don't cache credentials yourself.

## 21. 🔧 Going deeper: one pipeline that honours most factors

```yaml title=".github/workflows/deploy.yml"
name: deploy
on:
  push: { branches: [main] }
permissions: { id-token: write, contents: read }
jobs:
  ship:
    runs-on: ubuntu-latest
    steps:
      - uses: actions/checkout@v4                       # I: one codebase, a specific commit
      - uses: actions/setup-node@v4
        with: { node-version-file: .nvmrc, cache: npm }
      - run: npm ci && npm test && npm run build          # II: lock-file install
      - uses: aws-actions/configure-aws-credentials@v4
        with: { role-to-assume: arn:aws:iam::123456789012:role/gha-deploy, aws-region: eu-west-1 }
      - uses: aws-actions/amazon-ecr-login@v2
        id: ecr
      - name: Build once (V: build has no config)
        run: |
          IMAGE=${{ steps.ecr.outputs.registry }}/api:${{ github.sha }}
          docker build --build-arg GIT_SHA=${{ github.sha }} -t $IMAGE .
          docker push $IMAGE
      - name: Migrate as a one-off process (XII)
        run: ./scripts/run-task.sh "npm run migration:run" ${{ github.sha }}
      - name: Release + run (V, IX: rolling, health-checked)
        run: ./scripts/release.sh ${{ github.sha }}     # new task-def revision with prod config, update service
```

## Recap

- 🎓 The twelve factors are a **contract between a networked service and its platform**, written to stop snowflake servers and erosion. The scorecard (section 19) sums up how each one aged.
- ➕ **Serverless (section 10):** module scope survives warm invocations (Factor VI), published versions and aliases are releases (V), reserved concurrency is the formation (VIII), there's no shutdown hook so flush before returning (IX), admin tasks are their own synchronous invocation (XII).
- ➕ **Schedulers and workers (section 12):** `replicas: 1` isn't exactly one, so fence each tick in the database. CronJobs run about once per schedule, so tasks stay idempotent. Scale workers on backlog, not CPU.
- ➕ **Safe migrations (section 17):** one runner before the rollout, `lock_timeout` against the lock queue, the non-blocking DDL forms, and backfills as batched, resumable one-off jobs.
- ➕ **Beyond twelve (section 20):** API first with a breaking-change gate, telemetry exported to a configured endpoint, every call authenticated, and workload identity instead of static credentials.

## Interview questions

1. **What is the Twelve-Factor App, in one line?** **A contract between a networked service and the platform running it, so any machine, platform or teammate can run, scale and update it without surprises.**
2. **What is software erosion?** **An app breaking with no code change, because its hand-built environment changed underneath it (OS patches, neighbour apps' libraries, departed knowledge).**
3. **What makes two deploys of the same app different?** **Only config (and possibly which commit they run). The code comes from one codebase.**
4. **Are monorepos a Factor I violation?** **No. Each app built from the monorepo is its own codebase; the violation is one app's code copied into two places.**
5. **Why do you need both a manifest and isolation?** **The manifest declares what you need, isolation guarantees nothing else from the host leaks in. Either one alone still runs the wrong code.**
6. **Give a test for whether config is in your code.** **Could you open-source the repo right now without leaking a credential?**
7. **Why might env vars be wrong for secrets?** **They leak: inherited by child processes, dumped by crash reporters and debug pages, readable via `/proc/PID/environ`. Use a secret manager, read at boot into memory.**
8. **Why not `config/environments/staging.rb`?** **Named environments don't scale; each new deploy needs code. Granular env vars let you compose any deploy by setting values.**
9. **What is a resource handle?** **The URL plus credentials for a backing service, e.g. `postgres://user:pass@host:5432/db`, stored in config so services are swappable without code changes.**
10. **How does build/release/run make rollback instant?** **Builds are immutable and releases (build + config) form an append-only ledger, so rolling back is re-running an earlier release with nothing to rebuild.**
11. **Why are sticky sessions a violation?** **They tie users to one instance: killing it logs them out and new instances get no users, defeating horizontal scale. Put sessions in Redis or Postgres.**
12. **When is it acceptable to hold state in a process?** **Per-transaction caches and temp files; and real-time connections like WebSockets, where the feature is the state (bridged with pub/sub).**
13. **Why should the unit of scaling be the process, not the thread?** **Threads are bounded by one machine; share-nothing processes scale across machines.**
14. **Why does boot time matter?** **60 s boot means a 10-minute rolling deploy over 10 instances and an autoscaler a minute late.**
15. **How do you make a worker safe to kill mid-job?** **Return the job to the queue and make jobs re-entrant: transactional or idempotent, so a re-run is harmless.**
16. **Give a dev/prod parity bug.** **SQLite's `LIKE` is case-insensitive for ASCII, Postgres's is case-sensitive, so search silently behaves differently.**
17. **Why should an app write logs to stdout instead of a file?** **The platform routes and merges the stream; the app stays environment-agnostic and streams from all instances become one time-ordered log.**
18. **How should a migration run?** **As a one-off process from the same release (same image and config) as the app, not via SSH.**
19. ➕ **Why can a module-level variable in a Lambda function leak data between users?** **The execution environment is frozen and reused between invocations, so module scope survives across requests, but only within that one environment. Keep only shared clients and caches there; per-request state stays in the handler.**
20. ➕ **What plays the role of a Factor V release on Lambda?** **A published version: an immutable snapshot of code and config. An alias points at it; rollback moves the alias, and a weighted alias gives a two-version canary.**
21. ➕ **Your scheduler has `replicas: 1`. Why can the nightly job still run twice?** **A rolling update surges a second copy before the old one stops, and a partitioned node can keep running while a replacement starts. Fence each tick in the database (a unique key per job and scheduled time) and keep the job idempotent.**
22. ➕ **Is a Kubernetes CronJob with `concurrencyPolicy: Forbid` exactly-once?** **No. Forbid only prevents overlapping runs. The controller creates a Job about once per schedule and can create two or none, so the task still needs its own fence and idempotency.**
23. ➕ **Why scale workers on queue depth instead of CPU?** **I/O-bound workers sit at low CPU while the backlog grows. Queue length (for example with KEDA) measures the waiting work directly.**
24. ➕ **Why shouldn't migrations run at app boot?** **Every replica races to run them, boot stops being fast, probes can kill a pod mid-migration, and the web role needs DDL rights. Run one migration job before the rollout and stop the deploy if it fails.**
25. ➕ **How can a 1 ms `ALTER TABLE` cause an outage, and how do you prevent it?** **It waits for an `ACCESS EXCLUSIVE` lock behind a long query, and every new query queues behind it. Set `lock_timeout` so it fails fast, then retry.**
26. ➕ **How do you add a foreign key to a large live table without blocking writes?** **Add it `NOT VALID` (instant, enforced for new rows), then `VALIDATE CONSTRAINT`, which only takes a `SHARE UPDATE EXCLUSIVE` lock. Build the supporting index with `CREATE INDEX CONCURRENTLY` outside a transaction.**
27. ➕ **Why move a backfill out of the migration?** **One huge `UPDATE` is one transaction: long locks, WAL bloat, replica lag, and all work lost if interrupted. A separate one-off job in small committed batches is resumable and idempotent.**
28. ➕ **Which factors does "Beyond the Twelve-Factor App" add?** **API first, telemetry, and authentication and authorization (15 factors in total, Kevin Hoffman, 2016).**
29. ➕ **What is workload identity, and how does it replace a database password?** **The platform gives the process an identity (task role, Pod Identity) that it exchanges for short-lived credentials. With RDS IAM auth the app signs a 15-minute token per new connection instead of holding a static password.**
